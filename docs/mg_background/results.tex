\paragraph{Numerical setup.} The gauge configurations are quenched, generated with the
plaquette gauge action at $\beta=6.0$, which corresponds to a lattice spacing of about
$0.1$~fm; the volumes are $12^4$, $16^4$, $24^4$ and $24^3\times48$, one configuration each.
On them two setups of the M\"obius operator are used (Table~\ref{tab:setups}), both with
$(b,c)=(1.5,0.5)$, the choice of the JLQCD and the RBC/UKQCD Collaborations, and $L_s=8$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Setup B (bare links).} The domain-wall height $M_5=1.8$ is the value typical of
practical simulations on unsmeared links, as adopted by the RBC/UKQCD Collaboration: on rough
fields the lattice artifact of the Wilson kernel shifts the best value of $M_5$ above~$1$.
\item \emph{Setup S (stout links).} Three steps of the stout smearing of
Sec.~\ref{sec:smearing} with $\rho=0.1$ are applied to the links of the fermion operator, the
setup adopted by the JLQCD Collaboration. Then $M_5=1.0$ is used, since the smearing largely
suppresses the artifact that shifts the best value of $M_5$ away from $1$.
\end{itemize}
The quark mass ranges over $m=0.001$--$0.1$, and the Pauli--Villars mass is $1$. Timings are
from an NVIDIA A30; an RTX~3080 was used for iteration counts and spectral quantities only.
The implementation is described in Appendix~\ref{sec:impl}.

\paragraph{What the two setups mean for the multigrid.} The residual breaking of chiral
symmetry at finite $L_s$ comes from the eigenvalues near zero of the M\"obius kernel
$H_M=\gamma_5(b+c)W\big(2+(b-c)W\big)^{-1}$~\cite{BNO}, that is from near-zero modes of
$W=D_W(-M_5)$, which on rough gauge fields come from small dislocations. The two setups keep
them small in two ways, smoothing the field (S) or moving $M_5$ (B), and so give the
multigrid low modes of different character. It shows in three measured places: the smoother
needs the interval $[\lambda_{\rm hi}/10,\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ in~S and
$[\lambda_{\rm hi}/30,\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ in~B, and with B's interval setup~S stagnates, with
S's setup~B needs nearly three times the iterations (Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}); the field of
values of $\AR$ reaches less far below zero with smearing ($\Re W\ge-4.6\times10^{-3}$
against $-5.0\times10^{-2}$ on $12^4$, Sec.~\ref{sec:spectra}); and the test vectors of $\AR$
are much closer to null vectors of $\AR\dg$ in S than in B ($\|\AR\dg y\|^2/\|y\|^2\approx0.015$
against $0.27$ on $12^4$ and $16^4$, while $\|\AR y\|^2/\|y\|^2\approx0.003$ in both), which is why the dual
cycle works in~B but stalls in~S (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}, Table~\ref{tab:c4}).

\subsection{Field of values: the coarse correction amplifies}
\label{sec:fov}

The next four subsections ask why the method converges although the usual theory
does not apply. Two notions from non-Hermitian linear algebra are used. The \emph{field
of values} $W(X)=\{z\dg Xz/z\dg z\}$ contains the spectrum, and its leftmost point is the
smallest eigenvalue of the Hermitian part $(X+X\dg)/2$; GCR is guaranteed to converge at
every restart length when $W$ lies in the right half-plane~\cite{ESS}. The
\emph{$\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum} is the set of $z$ where $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)<\varepsilon$,
i.e.\ where $\|(z-X)^{-1}\|>1/\varepsilon$; for a normal $X$ it is the
$\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the spectrum, for a non-normal one it can be much larger,
and it bounds the convergence of full GMRES~\cite{TrefethenEmbree}.

Why the eigenvalues alone do not decide, for readers used to Hermitian matrices: the
eigenvectors of a non-normal matrix are not orthogonal, and can be nearly parallel. A vector
then decomposes into eigenvectors with large coefficients of opposite sign that cancel; each
component decays as its eigenvalue says, but the cancellation is lost along the way, and
$\|p(X)z\|$ can grow for a while although every eigenvalue lies in the right half-plane. The
field of values sees this ($z\dg Xz$ can be negative for such a $z$), and so does the
pseudospectrum. How nearly parallel an eigenvector is to the others is measured by the
condition number of its eigenvalue, $\kappa=1/|y\dg x|$ for the right eigenvector $x$ and
the left eigenvector $y$ normalized to $\|x\|=\|y\|=1$: $\kappa=1$ for a normal matrix,
and large $\kappa$ means the eigenvalue moves by $\kappa\varepsilon$ under a perturbation of
size $\varepsilon$, which is what widens the pseudospectrum around it
(Secs.~\ref{sec:spectrum}, \ref{sec:pseudo}).

The coarse correction amplifies some vectors by $10^3$--$10^4$, and
the field of values of the preconditioned operator reaches into the left half-plane
(this subsection), so the bound of Eisenstat, Elman and Schultz~\cite{ESS} for GCR is
lost. Its eigenvalues nevertheless stay at $\Re\lambda\ge0.78$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:spectrum}), and its pseudospectra bound the convergence of full GMRES
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pseudo}). The truncated GCR used in practice is not covered by that
bound: it converges best when the coarse solves are inexact, and with accurate coarse
solves it needs more iterations (setup~B) or stagnates (setup~S; Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}).

The Hermitian part of $\AR\mathcal M$ was computed with $\mathcal M$ made a fixed
linear map: both coarse factors solved to $|r|^2/|b|^2<10^{-20}$ in double precision
with the exact adjoints, and $\mathcal M\dg$ built with the same $\Pro$ and the
same smoother interval, so that $\langle z,\AR\mathcal M w\rangle$ and
$\langle\mathcal M\dg\AR\dg z,w\rangle$ agree to $10^{-10}$. The cycle was also
taken apart: the smoother alone, $S=p(\AR)$, the coarse correction alone, $E$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr}, and the cycle $\mathcal M$ (Table~\ref{tab:fov}).

The coarse correction is what pushes the field of values into the left half-plane. On
$4^3\times8$ the field of values of $\AR\mathcal M$ lies in the right half-plane; on $12^4$ and
$16^4$ it does not. Table~\ref{tab:fov} shows where the change comes from.
\begin{itemize}
\item The field of values of $\AR$ itself hardly depends on the volume. From $12^4$ to $24^4$ its
upper edge moves by less than $1\%$, and its lower edge goes from $-4.6\times10^{-3}$ to
$-1.3\times10^{-2}$ (S) and stays at $-5\times10^{-2}$ (B).
\item The smoother is harmless: $\|I-\AR S\|\approx1$.
\item The coarse correction amplifies: $\|I-\AR E\|$ is $10^3$--$10^4$, against $18.5$ on
$4^3\times8$. $\AR E$ is the identity on the vectors the correction is built from
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}), but it amplifies other directions by three to four orders of
magnitude, like a strongly oblique projection.
\end{itemize}
Eick and Mawhinney~\cite{EickMawhinney} describe the same mechanism for a renormalization-group
coarse lattice. The coarse inverse is large on the low modes of the coarse operator. The
interpolated correction is not exactly a low mode of the fine operator, and the part that misses
comes back amplified. In their case the fine operator is $D\dg D$, whose spectrum reaches $520$
against $0.05$ for its lowest modes; it supplies the four orders of magnitude, and they need a
Chebyshev filter of degree $150$--$300$. In our case $\AR$ is close to the identity on the high
modes, because the Pauli--Villars operator divides them out; its field of values ends at $2$ (S)
and $4.5$ (B). The large factor is the coarse inverse itself. The post-smoothing, a Chebyshev
polynomial of degree $7$--$11$ on the upper part of the spectrum, acts as the filter, and the
whole cycle keeps $\|I-\AR\mathcal M\|$ at $55$ (S) and $5.6$ (B). We have not separated how much
the post-smoothing removes.

So the GCR bound of Ref.~\cite{ESS} does not apply. The field of values is also not what decides
the iteration count. If we replace $E$ by the Galerkin correction of $\AR$ itself,
$\Pro(\Pro\dg\AR\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg$ (built densely), the field of values of setup~B moves into the
right half-plane, $[0.121,1.99]$, and $\|I-\AR\mathcal M\|$ drops to $1.84$ (setup~S:
$[-3.62,13.1]$, $16.8$). Yet GCR(8) to $10^{-16}$ then needs more iterations: $16$ against $13$
(B) and $12$ against $10$ (S).

\subsection{Pseudospectra and a bound for GMRES}
\label{sec:pseudo}

For a non-normal operator the eigenvalues alone do not bound the convergence of
GMRES; the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum, the set where $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)<\varepsilon$,
does~\cite{NRT,TrefethenEmbree}. If a contour $\Gamma$ of length $L$ encloses the
spectrum of $X$ but not the origin and $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)\ge\varepsilon$ on it, then
for any polynomial $p_k$ with $p_k(0)=1$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\|p_k(X)\|\le\frac{L}{2\pi\varepsilon}\max_{z\in\Gamma}|p_k(z)| .
\label{eq:pseudobound}
\end{equation}
An isolated outlier $\lambda_o$ outside $\Gamma$ is removed by the factor
$(1-z/\lambda_o)$ of $p_k$: then $p_k(X)=p_k(X)(I-P_o)$ with $P_o$ the spectral
projector, $\|(z-X)^{-1}(I-P_o)\|\le\kappa_o/\varepsilon$, and with a circle
$|z-c|=r$ and $p_k(z)=(1-z/\lambda_o)(1-z/c)^{k-1}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\kappa_o\,\frac{r}{\varepsilon}\,
\frac{|\lambda_o-c|+r}{|\lambda_o|}\Big(\frac rc\Big)^{k-1}.
\label{eq:outlierbound}
\end{equation}

On $16^4$ setup~S has two outliers; the factor $\kappa_o$ is then replaced by
$\|I-P_1-P_2\|\le1+\kappa_1+\kappa_2$ and there are two factors of the
form $(|\lambda_o-c|+r)/|\lambda_o|$. On the real axis between the origin and the
spectrum, $\sigma_{\min}(z-\AR\mathcal M)\ge0.024$ (S) and $\ge0.031$ (B) on
$12^4$ ($z=-0.5\ldots0.7$), and $0.107$ (S), $0.271$ (B) at the origin.

The bound guarantees convergence at a fixed rate, $r/c$ per iteration, with no stagnation. It
is pessimistic by a factor of $3$--$5$ in iterations: GCR needs $10$--$17$ with exact coarse
solves (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}). Its rate, however, is the one we measure on $24^3\times48$. There
the median factor per GCR step of $|r|^2$ is $0.23$--$0.34$ (Table~\ref{tab:history}), that is
$0.48$--$0.58$ in $|r|$, against $r/c=0.5$--$0.6$ for setup~S. Setup~B does not get worse from
$12^4$ to $16^4$ ($\min\sigma$ grows from $0.098$ to $0.13$). In setup~S the outliers grow, but
their condition numbers fall and $\varepsilon$ stays the same.

\paragraph{What is not yet rigorous.} (i) With 16 points per circle the spacing is
larger than $\varepsilon$, and the sampled minimum says nothing about the points in
between. On the two dense circles of $12^4$ the spacing is below $\varepsilon$, and since
$\sigma_{\min}(z-X)$ is $1$-Lipschitz in $z$ the bound holds on the whole circle,
$\sigma_{\min}\ge0.0164$ (S) and $\ge0.0676$ (B); the guaranteed iterations change from
$53$ to $54$ and from $57$ to $58$. The circles of $16^4$ have only the 16 points.
(ii) Lanczos approaches $\sigma_{\min}^2$ from above; a rigorous lower bound needs
an inclusion theorem (Kato--Temple) with a bound on the second singular value.
(iii) That the disc holds all eigenvalues but the outliers rests on the four
eigenvalues farthest from $c$ found by Arnoldi. (iv) The bound is for full GMRES
with a fixed linear $\mathcal M$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}). (v) The spectra, and the
FOV of $\AR\mathcal M$, are not available on $24^4$ and $24^3\times48$: the
eigensolvers do not fit in the memory of the A30. This is a limit of the hardware
used here, not of the method.

Truncated GCR works only with inexact coarse solves. With the usual coarse solves to $10^{-2}$,
GCR(2) needs one to five iterations more than the full method (Table~\ref{tab:gcrk}). With
accurate coarse solves, where $\mathcal M$ is close to the fixed map of
Secs.~\ref{sec:fov}--\ref{sec:pseudo}, GCR(2) stagnates ($|r|^2\approx3\times10^{-8}$ after 1000
iterations), while GCR(8) converges in $26$ and GCR($\ge16$) in $17$. This matches the analysis:
the pseudospectra guarantee the full method, and a truncated method has no guarantee when the
field of values contains the origin~\cite{ESS,Embree}.

Why the truncated solver works with inexact coarse solves is an empirical fact, and the obvious
explanation fails. A coarse solve stopped at $10^{-2}$ does not leave out the directions in which
$E$ amplifies. Take the vector $x$ at the left end of the field of values of $\AR\mathcal M$
(exact coarse solves, $12^4$). With coarse solves to $10^{-2}$ the cycle gives
$\Re\,x\dg\AR\mathcal Mx=-25.2$ (S) and $-1.43$ (B), against $-24.3$ and $-1.49$ with exact
ones, and $\|(I-\AR E)x\|=1.16\times10^3$ and $1.03\times10^2$, against $1.13\times10^3$ and
$1.12\times10^2$. With tolerances $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-4}$ they agree with the exact solves to within
$1\%$. What the tolerance changes is that the cycle is no longer a fixed linear map: with Krylov
coarse solves stopped early, it depends on its input.

On $24^3\times48$ the full method cannot run on the A30. GCR(8) and GCR(16) run out of device
memory after the setup: GCR(2) peaks at $19.9$ of $24$\,GB, and every further direction is
another fine vector.

\paragraph{What this establishes.} As usual for multigrid preconditioners of lattice Dirac
operators, the convergence of the truncated flexible outer method is established numerically.
GCR(2) with coarse solves stopped at $10^{-2}$ converged in every run of this section, without
plateaus, at the rate the pseudospectral bound of Sec.~\ref{sec:pseudo} gives for the full
method. No guarantee is available for a truncated method whose preconditioner varies from step to
step. That accurate coarse solves make GCR(2) slower, and stagnate in setup~S, is an observation of
its own. Since the loose solves do not avoid the amplifying directions, the variation of the cycle
with its input is the candidate explanation. A fixed but inaccurate linear coarse solve, or the
exact cycle perturbed at random, would test it.

\subsection{In HMC}
\label{sec:hmcresults}

In HMC the multigrid solves the normal equation of the even-odd Ratio action, $(K\dg K)^{-1}$,
as two solves, one with $D$ and one with $D\dg$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}), repeated on the residual
until it is small enough. The test vectors are kept from solve to solve and improved by a
bootstrap when a trajectory starts; P and the coarse operators are kept until the iterations
grow by half. All runs are in setup~S with Neff's $\alpha=0.6$ for both solvers, on the A30.

\paragraph{The same trajectories.} A dynamical $16^4$ configuration was thermalized from the
quenched one by mixed-precision CG: 60 trajectories at $m=0.05$ (acceptance $0.98$,
$\langle e^{-\Delta H}\rangle=1.002$), then 40 at $m=0.01$ (acceptance $0.98$,
$\langle e^{-\Delta H}\rangle=0.993$), trajectories of length~1 with Omelyan steps
$[20,2]$; 10 steps were unstable at $m=0.01$ ($\Delta H\approx10^3$ with $\alpha=0.6$ and
$\alpha=1$ alike). From it, five trajectories with the multigrid reproduce those of CG: the
plaquette to $10^{-11}$, $\Delta H$ to three digits.

\paragraph{Cost.} On this lattice the multigrid is the slower solver (Table~\ref{tab:hmc16}).
CG needs about 300 iterations a solve at $m=0.01$ and still about 300 at $m=0.001$: on a
dynamical configuration of this size the smallest eigenvalues are held up by the finite
volume, not by the mass. The multigrid needs about 55 iterations, but one of them, a whole
cycle, costs about as much as 24 of CG. Varying the smoother (4, 6 or 8 steps), the coarse and
the inner tolerances, the bootstrap rounds and the directions of GCR changed its time by at most
$6\%$. On the quenched $24^4$ configuration at $m=0.001$ the picture is the opposite: CG needs
about 6400 iterations a solve, and the multigrid, setup included, is faster on a short trajectory
(203\,s against 264\,s). But a trajectory of length~1 there blows up ($\Delta H\approx4\times10^9$),
so that configuration is no test of HMC. Data that show the multigrid faster in HMC therefore do
not exist yet: the dynamical volumes thermalized here are limited by the finite volume. They need
a volume where CG takes more than about 1300 iterations a solve.

Fewer iterations are not always less time (Table~\ref{tab:costs}): $2^4$ aggregates
need $8$--$9$ iterations but a larger coarse lattice, so the solve and the setup take
about twice as long as with $4^4$; $48$ test vectors save a third of the iterations and
lose in setup and in time per iteration.
For Wilson fermions, Whyte, Stathopoulos and Romero~\cite{WhyteSVD} obtain the effect of
more test vectors at the coarse size of fewer: they generate more, truncate them by a
singular value decomposition on each aggregate and chirality (as $\Pro$ is split here
by $\Gfive$), and stream the decomposition to save device memory. Applied here, the
truncated basis would no longer contain $s$ and $\hat t$ exactly, so the exactness of
Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} would be lost; whether the gain outweighs that has not been
tried.
With the setup as fast as it is now, the base setting is no longer the time optimum on
$12^4$: $16$ test vectors (a coarse dimension of $32$) halve the setup at a solve that is
no slower, and more smoother steps ($\nu=12$, $16$ for setup~S) solve faster at the same
setup. On $24^3\times48$ the fewer test vectors do not carry over (setup~S, $m=0.001$,
point source, A30, \texttt{6d5bf18}): $16$ halve the setup there too ($116$ against

at the cap of $2000$; $24$ ($184$\,s of setup) need $162$ iterations for the first column,
against $29$--$32$ with $32$. More smoother steps have not been tried there. All runs use one setup seed, and the iterations move by a few
between builds that add in other orders, so differences of a few iterations are within
that variation.
