\subsection{Field of values: the coarse correction amplifies}
\label{sec:fov}

The next four subsections ask why the method converges although the usual theory
does not apply. Two notions from non-Hermitian linear algebra are used. The \emph{field
of values} $W(X)=\{z\dg Xz/z\dg z\}$ contains the spectrum, and its leftmost point is the
smallest eigenvalue of the Hermitian part $(X+X\dg)/2$; GCR is guaranteed to converge at
every restart length when $W$ lies in the right half-plane~\cite{ESS}. The
\emph{$\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum} is the set of $z$ where $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)<\varepsilon$,
i.e.\ where $\|(z-X)^{-1}\|>1/\varepsilon$; for a normal $X$ it is the
$\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the spectrum, for a non-normal one it can be much larger,
and it bounds the convergence of full GMRES~\cite{TrefethenEmbree}.

The coarse correction amplifies some vectors by $10^3$--$10^4$, and
the field of values of the preconditioned operator reaches into the left half-plane
(this subsection), so the bound of Eisenstat, Elman and Schultz~\cite{ESS} for GCR is
lost. Its eigenvalues nevertheless stay at $\Re\lambda\ge0.78$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:spectrum}), and its pseudospectra bound the convergence of full GMRES
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pseudo}). The truncated GCR used in practice is not covered by that
bound: it converges best when the coarse solves are inexact, and with accurate coarse
solves it needs more iterations (setup~B) or stagnates (setup~S; Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}).

The Hermitian part of $\AR\mathcal M$ was computed with $\mathcal M$ made a fixed
linear map: both coarse factors solved to $|r|^2/|b|^2<10^{-20}$ in double precision
with the exact adjoints, and $\mathcal M\dg$ built with the same $\Pro$ and the
same smoother interval, so that $\langle z,\AR\mathcal M w\rangle$ and
$\langle\mathcal M\dg\AR\dg z,w\rangle$ agree to $10^{-10}$. The cycle was also
taken apart: the smoother alone, $S=p(\AR)$, the coarse correction alone, $E$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr}, and the cycle $\mathcal M$ (Table~\ref{tab:fov}).

On $4^3\times8$ the field of values of $\AR\mathcal M$ lies in the right half-plane;
on $12^4$ and $16^4$ it does not. The field of values of $\AR$ itself hardly moves
with the volume: from $12^4$ to $24^4$ its upper edge moves by less than $1\%$ and its lower edge
goes from $-4.6\times10^{-3}$ to $-1.3\times10^{-2}$ (S) and stays at $-5\times10^{-2}$ (B). The smoother is harmless ($\|I-\AR S\|\approx1$);
the factor that amplifies is the coarse correction, $\|I-\AR E\|$ of order $10^3$
to $10^4$ against $18.5$ on $4^3\times8$: $\AR E$ is the identity on the vectors the
correction is built from (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}) but amplifies other directions
by three to four orders of magnitude, like a strongly oblique projection. The mechanism
is the one Eick and Mawhinney~\cite{EickMawhinney} describe for a renormalization-group
coarse lattice of M\"obius fermions: the coarse inverse is large on the low modes of the
coarse operator, the interpolated correction is not exactly a low mode of the fine
operator, and the part that misses comes back amplified. There the fine operator is
$D\dg D$, whose spectrum reaches $520$ against $0.05$ for its lowest thousand modes; it
supplies the four orders of magnitude, and a Chebyshev low-pass filter of degree
$150$--$300$ is needed. Here $\AR$, with the Pauli--Villars operator dividing out the high
modes, is close to the identity on them, and its field of values ends at $2$ (S) and
$4.5$ (B); the large factor is the coarse inverse itself. The post-smoothing, a Chebyshev
polynomial of degree $7$--$11$ on the upper part of the spectrum, plays the role of the
filter, and the cycle as a whole keeps $\|I-\AR\mathcal M\|$ at $55$ (S) and $5.6$ (B);
how much of the remaining amplification the post-smoothing removes has not been
separated. The bound of
Ref.~\cite{ESS} for GCR therefore does not apply (Sec.~\ref{sec:spectra}).
Replacing $E$ by the Galerkin correction of $\AR$ itself,
$\Pro(\Pro\dg\AR\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg$ (built densely), brings the field of values of setup~B
into the right half-plane, $[0.121,1.99]$, and reduces $\|I-\AR\mathcal M\|$ to
$1.84$ (setup~S: $[-3.62,13.1]$, $16.8$); but GCR(8) to $10^{-16}$ then needs more
iterations, $16$ against $13$ (B) and $12$ against $10$ (S). The field of values is
not what decides the iteration count.

\subsection{Pseudospectra and a bound for GMRES}
\label{sec:pseudo}

For a non-normal operator the eigenvalues alone do not bound the convergence of
GMRES; the $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum, the set where $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)<\varepsilon$,
does~\cite{NRT,TrefethenEmbree}. If a contour $\Gamma$ of length $L$ encloses the
spectrum of $X$ but not the origin and $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)\ge\varepsilon$ on it, then
for any polynomial $p_k$ with $p_k(0)=1$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\|p_k(X)\|\le\frac{L}{2\pi\varepsilon}\max_{z\in\Gamma}|p_k(z)| .
\label{eq:pseudobound}
\end{equation}
An isolated outlier $\lambda_o$ outside $\Gamma$ is removed by the factor
$(1-z/\lambda_o)$ of $p_k$: then $p_k(X)=p_k(X)(I-P_o)$ with $P_o$ the spectral
projector, $\|(z-X)^{-1}(I-P_o)\|\le\kappa_o/\varepsilon$, and with a circle
$|z-c|=r$ and $p_k(z)=(1-z/\lambda_o)(1-z/c)^{k-1}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\kappa_o\,\frac{r}{\varepsilon}\,
\frac{|\lambda_o-c|+r}{|\lambda_o|}\Big(\frac rc\Big)^{k-1}.
\label{eq:outlierbound}
\end{equation}

On $16^4$ setup~S has two outliers; the factor $\kappa_o$ is then replaced by
$\|I-P_1-P_2\|\le1+\kappa_1+\kappa_2$ and there are two factors of the
form $(|\lambda_o-c|+r)/|\lambda_o|$. On the real axis between the origin and the
spectrum, $\sigma_{\min}(z-\AR\mathcal M)\ge0.024$ (S) and $\ge0.031$ (B) on
$12^4$ ($z=-0.5\ldots0.7$), and $0.107$ (S), $0.271$ (B) at the origin.

The bound guarantees convergence at a fixed rate, $r/c$ per iteration, without
stagnation; it is pessimistic by a factor of $3$--$5$ in iterations (GCR needs
$10$--$17$ with exact coarse solves, Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}), and its rate is that
measured on $24^3\times48$, where the median factor per GCR step of $|r|^2$ is
$0.23$--$0.34$ (Table~\ref{tab:history}), i.e.\ $0.48$--$0.58$ in $|r|$, against
$r/c=0.5$--$0.6$ for setup~S. Setup~B is not worse between $12^4$ and $16^4$
($\min\sigma$ grows from $0.098$ to $0.13$); in setup~S the outliers grow but
their condition numbers fall and $\varepsilon$ stays the same.

\paragraph{What is not yet rigorous.} (i) With 16 points per circle the spacing is
larger than $\varepsilon$, and the sampled minimum says nothing about the points in
between. On the two dense circles of $12^4$ the spacing is below $\varepsilon$, and since
$\sigma_{\min}(z-X)$ is $1$-Lipschitz in $z$ the bound holds on the whole circle,
$\sigma_{\min}\ge0.0164$ (S) and $\ge0.0676$ (B); the guaranteed iterations change from
$53$ to $54$ and from $57$ to $58$. The circles of $16^4$ have only the 16 points.
(ii) Lanczos approaches $\sigma_{\min}^2$ from above; a rigorous lower bound needs
an inclusion theorem (Kato--Temple) with a bound on the second singular value.
(iii) That the disc holds all eigenvalues but the outliers rests on the four
eigenvalues farthest from $c$ found by Arnoldi. (iv) The bound is for full GMRES
with a fixed linear $\mathcal M$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}). (v) The spectra, and the
FOV of $\AR\mathcal M$, are not available on $24^4$ and $24^3\times48$: the
eigensolvers do not fit in the memory of the A30. This is a limit of the hardware
used here, not of the method.

With the usual coarse solves to $10^{-2}$, GCR(2) needs one to five iterations more
than the full method (Table~\ref{tab:gcrk}). With accurate coarse solves, where
$\mathcal M$ is close to the fixed map of Secs.~\ref{sec:fov}--\ref{sec:pseudo},
GCR(2) stagnates ($|r|^2\approx3\times10^{-8}$ after 1000 iterations) while GCR(8)
converges in $26$ and GCR($\ge16$) in $17$. This is what the analysis predicts:
the pseudospectra guarantee the full method; a truncated or restarted one has no
such guarantee when the field of values contains the origin~\cite{ESS,Embree}. That
the truncated solver works with inexact coarse solves is an empirical fact, and the
obvious explanation does not hold: a coarse solve stopped at $10^{-2}$ does not leave out
the directions in which $E$ amplifies. On the vector $x$ at the left end of the field of
values of $\AR\mathcal M$ (exact coarse solves; $12^4$, \texttt{preconditioned\_fov\_device
--parts}), the cycle with coarse solves to $10^{-2}$ gives $\Re\,x\dg\AR\mathcal Mx=-25.2$
(S) and $-1.43$ (B), against $-24.3$ and $-1.49$ with exact ones, and
$\|(I-\AR E)x\|=1.16\times10^3$ and $1.03\times10^2$, against $1.13\times10^3$ and
$1.12\times10^2$; with tolerances $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-4}$ they are those of the exact solves
to within $1\%$. What the tolerance changes is that the cycle is no longer a fixed
linear map: with Krylov coarse solves stopped early it depends on its input.
On $24^3\times48$ the full method cannot be run on the A30: GCR(8) and GCR(16) run
out of device memory after the setup (GCR(2) peaks at $19.9$ of $24$\,GB, and every
further direction is another fine-grid vector).

\paragraph{What this establishes.} As is usual for multigrid preconditioners of lattice
Dirac operators, the convergence of the truncated flexible outer method is established
numerically: GCR(2) with the coarse solves stopped at $10^{-2}$ converged in every run of
this section, without plateaus, at the rate that the pseudospectral bound of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pseudo} gives for the full method. A guarantee for a truncated method whose
preconditioner varies from step to step is not available for flexible Krylov methods with
multigrid preconditioners in general. That accurate coarse solves make GCR(2) slower, and
in setup~S stagnate, is an observation of its own; since the loose solves do not avoid the amplifying directions,
the variation of the cycle with its input is the candidate, which a fixed but inaccurate
linear coarse solve, or the exact cycle perturbed at random, would test.

Table~\ref{tab:massscan} and Fig.~\ref{fig:massscan} are the mass scan on $24^3\times48$. The CGNE iterations grow
by a factor of $21$ (S) and $18$ (B) from $m=0.1$ to $m=0.001$, the multigrid
iterations by $2.2$ (S) and $3.7$ (B). At $m=0.001$ the multigrid solve is $7.5$ times
(S) and $9.0$ times (B) faster than the double-precision CGNE; with the setup of about
$210$\,s it pays for itself after $2$--$3$ right-hand sides, i.e.\ within one
point-source propagator (12 columns) for $m\le0.01$. At $m=0.1$ in setup~S the
multigrid is not faster at all. The multigrid needs three times the device memory of
CGNE; on the $24$\,GB A30 this excludes GCR($k$) with $k\ge8$ (out of memory after the
setup). Neff's $\alpha=0.6$ against $\alpha=1$ in the left form: equal within one
iteration for S except at $m=0.1$ (three fewer), two to three iterations fewer for B.
Against an earlier build (\texttt{535c9ef}) the iteration counts differ by one or two
in six of the 28 multigrid runs; the cause (code change or non-determinism of the
device reductions) is not established.

\paragraph{Mixed-precision CGNE.} For a comparison on equal terms the reference
solver also runs in mixed precision: CG in single precision on the normal equation of
$\Ce^{-1}S$, with reliable updates in double precision (the true residual recomputed
whenever $|r|^2$ has fallen by $10^{-2}$). It needs the iterations of double precision
(at $m=0.001$ in setup~S $14\%$ more) and $0.54$--$0.61$ times the time, with the same
final residuals and $7.2$\,GB of device memory. Against it the multigrid solve at
$m=0.001$ is $4.5$ (S) and $4.8$ (B) times faster, and the setup is paid back after
$4$--$5$ right-hand sides, within one propagator for $m\le0.005$; for $m\ge0.05$ (S)
the mixed-precision CGNE solve is the faster one, and at $m=0.1$ (B) the two take
nearly the same time.

\paragraph{A point-source propagator.} The break-even estimates are confirmed by
whole propagators, the $12$ columns of a point source (Table~\ref{tab:pointsource}).
At $m=0.001$ the multigrid takes half the wall time of the mixed-precision CGNE
including its setup, the solve per column being $4.5$--$5$ times faster; at $m=0.01$ the
setup is not quite paid back within the 12 columns, as $N_{\rm rhs}\approx15$--$18$
(Table~\ref{tab:massscan}) predicts. A point source needs more iterations than a
random right-hand side, with either solver. The pseudoscalar correlators of the two
agree to $4\times10^{-8}$--$7\times10^{-7}$ at $t\le3$ and to $2\times10^{-6}$ at all
$48$ times. The multigrid stops on the residual of $D$ ($|D\psi-\eta|^2/|\eta|^2$ of
$1$--$2\times10^{-18}$), the CGNE on that of its normal equation ($6\times10^{-17}$ to
$3\times10^{-16}$). The device memory is $21.8$\,GB (multigrid) and $9.1$\,GB
(mixed-precision CGNE).

For $c=0$ the coarse operators are $\Sigma$-Hermitian to rounding; for $c\neq0$ they

Fewer iterations are not always less time (Table~\ref{tab:costs}): $2^4$ aggregates
need $8$--$9$ iterations but a larger coarse lattice, so the solve and the setup take
about twice as long as with $4^4$; $48$ test vectors save a third of the iterations and
lose in setup and in time per iteration.
For Wilson fermions, Whyte, Stathopoulos and Romero~\cite{WhyteSVD} obtain the effect of
more test vectors at the coarse size of fewer: they generate more, truncate them by a
singular value decomposition on each aggregate and chirality (as $\Pro$ is split here
by $\Gfive$), and stream the decomposition to save device memory. Applied here, the
truncated basis would no longer contain $s$ and $\hat t$ exactly, so the exactness of
Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} would be lost; whether the gain outweighs that has not been
tried.
With the setup as fast as it is now, the base setting is no longer the time optimum on
$12^4$: $16$ test vectors (a coarse dimension of $32$) halve the setup at a solve that is
no slower, and more smoother steps ($\nu=12$, $16$ for setup~S) solve faster at the same
setup. On $24^3\times48$ the fewer test vectors do not carry over (setup~S, $m=0.001$,
point source, A30, \texttt{fbc8f00}): $16$ halve the setup there too ($116$ against
$207$\,s), but the columns that converge need $910$--$940$ iterations and the others stop
at the cap of $2000$; $24$ ($184$\,s of setup) need $162$ iterations for the first column,
against $29$--$32$ with $32$. More smoother steps have not been tried there. All runs use one setup seed, and the iterations move by a few
between builds that add in other orders, so differences of a few iterations are within
that variation.
