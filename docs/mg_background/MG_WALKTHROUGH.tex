in the RutticeQCD repository.} (``the note'') states. The note gives the method and its results in compact form; here each formula

followed with nothing more than standard graduate coursework in lattice QCD. The sections follow the

\section*{The solver in one page}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The solver in one page}
The task is $D\psi=\eta$ for the M\"obius operator at a light quark mass. The solver goes through
the following steps; each later section derives one of them.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Even-odd reduction} (Sec.~\ref{sec:schur}). Eliminate the odd sites:
$S\psi_e=\eta'_e$ on the even sites, then $\psi_o=\Co^{-1}(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e)$. This needs
$C^{-1}$ exact and cheap (Sec.~\ref{sec:Cinv}).
\item \emph{Pauli--Villars preconditioning} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pvforms}). With $\psi_e=\Ce^{-1}y$ and
the factor $(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg$ from the left, solve $\AR y=\rho_R$, whose spectrum lies in the
right half-plane.
\item \emph{Outer solver} (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcr}). Flexible GCR(2) on $\AR$ (or $\AL$) with one
multigrid cycle $\mathcal M$ as right preconditioner per step.
\item \emph{One cycle} (Sec.~\ref{sec:cycle}). A Chebyshev smoother before and after the coarse
correction.
\item \emph{The coarse correction} (Sec.~\ref{sec:factor}). $\AR^{-1}$ factorizes into
$\Ce\,\pi D^{-1}\iota\,\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota\,\Cpve\dg$; each inverse is replaced by a solve on
the coarse lattice, the Pauli--Villars factor by CG on a regularized normal equation and the quark
factor by BiCGStab with further levels (Sec.~\ref{sec:coarsesolve}).
\item \emph{The coarse space} (Secs.~\ref{sec:agg}--\ref{sec:probe} and \ref{sec:filter}).
The setup finds vectors $y_j$ near the low modes of $\AR\dg\AR$ by a Chebyshev filter and a
bootstrap, lifts each to $s_j$ and $\hat t_j$ so that the correction is exact on their span
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exact}), and builds $\Pro$ and the coarse operators from them.
\item \emph{Solves with $D\dg$} (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}) reuse the same setup through
$\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$.
\item \emph{Why it converges} (Sec.~\ref{sec:why}): the spectrum and pseudospectra of $\tilde A\mathcal M$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The operator and Pauli--Villars preconditioning (note Sec.~II.B)}

\subsection{Ingredients}
\label{sec:ingr}
The field $\psi$ lives on the four-dimensional lattice and carries a fifth index
$s=0,\dots,L_s-1$; each $\psi_s$ is a Dirac field. The chiral projectors are
\begin{equation}
P_\pm=\frac{1\pm\gamma_5}{2},\qquad P_\pm^2=P_\pm,\qquad P_+P_-=0,\qquad P_++P_-=1 .
\end{equation}
The Wilson hopping term and the Wilson operator of mass $-M_5$ are
\begin{align}
(K\psi)(x)&=\sum_{\mu=1}^{4}\Big[(1-\gamma_\mu)U_\mu(x)\psi(x+\hat\mu)
+(1+\gamma_\mu)U_\mu\dg(x-\hat\mu)\psi(x-\hat\mu)\Big],\nonumber\\
W&=(4-M_5)-\tfrac12K .
\end{align}
$W$ acts on every slice $s$ alike and does not mix slices.


\subsection{The free spectrum of $W$}
\label{sec:free}
The spectrum of $W$ on the free field ($U_\mu=1$) is needed twice: for the spectra of the note
(Sec.~II.A) and for $D_-$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:dminus}). Take a plane wave $\psi(x)=e^{ip\cdot x}u$ with
a constant spinor $u$:
\begin{align}
(K\psi)(x)&=\sum_\mu\Big[(1-\gamma_\mu)e^{ip_\mu}+(1+\gamma_\mu)e^{-ip_\mu}\Big]\psi(x)\nonumber\\
&=\sum_\mu\Big[\big(e^{ip_\mu}+e^{-ip_\mu}\big)-\gamma_\mu\big(e^{ip_\mu}-e^{-ip_\mu}\big)\Big]\psi(x)\nonumber\\
&=\sum_\mu\Big[2\cos p_\mu-2i\gamma_\mu\sin p_\mu\Big]\psi(x),\nonumber\\
W\psi&=\Big[(4-M_5)-\sum_\mu\cos p_\mu+i\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\sin p_\mu\Big]\psi\nonumber\\
&=\Big[-M_5+\sum_\mu(1-\cos p_\mu)+i\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\sin p_\mu\Big]\psi .
\end{align}
The matrix $G=\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\sin p_\mu$ is Hermitian, and since the $\gamma_\mu$ anticommute,
\begin{equation}
G^2=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\gamma_\mu\gamma_\nu\sin p_\mu\sin p_\nu
=\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu^2\sin^2p_\mu+\sum_{\mu<\nu}\{\gamma_\mu,\gamma_\nu\}\sin p_\mu\sin p_\nu
=\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu ,
\end{equation}
so its eigenvalues are $\pm(\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu)^{1/2}$. The eigenvalues of $W$ are therefore
\begin{equation}
w(p)=-M_5+\sum_\mu(1-\cos p_\mu)\pm i\Big(\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu\Big)^{1/2}.
\label{eq:wfree}
\end{equation}
Their real parts run from $-M_5$ ($p=0$) to $8-M_5$ ($p_\mu=\pi$). A mode bound to the walls
exists only where the real part is negative. At the corner with $n$ of the four $p_\mu$ equal to
$\pi$ and the others $0$ the real part is $2n-M_5$, so for $0<M_5<2$ only the region around $p=0$
qualifies: the walls carry one light quark and no doublers.

\subsection{The operator in Brower--Neff--Orginos form (note Sec.~II.B.1)}
\label{sec:bno}
With $b$, $c$ the M\"obius parameters, define
\begin{equation}
D_+=1+bW,\qquad D_-=cW-1 .
\end{equation}
The operator at quark mass $m$ acts slice by slice as
\begin{equation}
(D\psi)_s=D_+\psi_s+D_-\big(\Pm\psi_{s+1}+\Pp\psi_{s-1}\big),
\qquad \psi_{L_s}\equiv-m\psi_0,\qquad \psi_{-1}\equiv-m\psi_{L_s-1}.
\label{eq:Dslice}
\end{equation}
For $L_s=4$ the matrix in $s$ is
\begin{equation}
D=\begin{pmatrix}
D_+ & D_-\Pm & 0 & -mD_-\Pp\\
D_-\Pp & D_+ & D_-\Pm & 0\\
0 & D_-\Pp & D_+ & D_-\Pm\\
-mD_-\Pm & 0 & D_-\Pp & D_+
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The physical quark field is $q=\Pm\psi_0+\Pp\psi_{L_s-1}$; the runs use $b=1.5$, $c=0.5$,
$L_s=8$.

\subsection{Splitting off the fifth-dimension hop}
\label{sec:split}
Define the hop in the fifth dimension, with a minus sign,
\begin{equation}
(Y\psi)_s=-\Pm\psi_{s+1}-\Pp\psi_{s-1}\qquad\text{(same wrap-around)}.
\end{equation}
Comparing with Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dslice}, the off-diagonal part of $D$ is $-D_-Y$, with $D_-$ on the
\emph{left}:
\begin{equation}
D=D_+-D_-Y .
\end{equation}
Now insert $D_\pm$ and then $W$, one substitution per line:
\begin{align}
D&=(1+bW)-(cW-1)Y\nonumber\\
&=1+bW-cWY+Y\nonumber\\
&=1+Y+W\,(b-cY)\nonumber\\
&=1+Y+\Big[(4-M_5)-\tfrac12K\Big](b-cY)\nonumber\\
&=\underbrace{\big[1+b(4-M_5)\big]}_{d_+}+\underbrace{\big[1-c(4-M_5)\big]}_{d_-}Y
-\tfrac12K\,(b-cY)\nonumber\\
&=C-\tfrac12K\,(b-cY),\qquad C\equiv d_++d_-Y .
\label{eq:Dsplit}
\end{align}
$C$ contains no four-dimensional hop: it acts within one site, as an $L_s\times L_s$ matrix in
$s$. All the gauge field sits in $-\frac12K(b-cY)$. The Pauli--Villars operator is the same
operator at unit mass,
\begin{equation}
\Dpv=D\big|_{m=1},\qquad \Cpv=C\big|_{m=1}.
\end{equation}


\subsection{The $\Gfive$-transformation (note Sec.~II.B.2)}
\label{sec:g5}
For the Wilson operator $\gamma_5W\gamma_5=W\dg$. Domain-wall fermions use
$\Gfive=\gamma_5R$, $(R\psi)_s=\psi_{L_s-1-s}$. We derive how each block transforms.

\paragraph{$K$.} Read off the matrix elements of $K$ between neighbors:
\begin{equation}
K_{x,x+\hat\mu}=(1-\gamma_\mu)U_\mu(x),\qquad K_{x+\hat\mu,x}=(1+\gamma_\mu)U_\mu\dg(x).
\end{equation}
The adjoint swaps the indices and conjugates each block (spin and color commute, and the
$\gamma_\mu$ are Hermitian):
\begin{align}
(K\dg)_{x,x+\hat\mu}&=\big(K_{x+\hat\mu,x}\big)\dg=U_\mu(x)\,(1+\gamma_\mu)=(1+\gamma_\mu)U_\mu(x),\nonumber\\
(K\dg)_{x+\hat\mu,x}&=\big(K_{x,x+\hat\mu}\big)\dg=(1-\gamma_\mu)U_\mu\dg(x).
\end{align}
So $K\dg$ is $K$ with $\gamma_\mu\to-\gamma_\mu$. Since $\gamma_5\gamma_\mu\gamma_5=-\gamma_\mu$,
\begin{equation}
\gamma_5K\gamma_5=K\dg,\qquad \gamma_5W\gamma_5=W\dg .
\end{equation}
$W$ acts on every slice alike, so it commutes with $R$: $\Gfive W\Gfive=W\dg$, and the same for
$D_\pm=$ functions of $W$: $\Gfive D_\pm\Gfive=D_\pm\dg$.

\paragraph{$Y$.} Write $Y$ as a matrix in $s$ ($L_s=4$), from $(Y\psi)_s=-\Pm\psi_{s+1}-\Pp\psi_{s-1}$
and the wrap-around $\psi_{L_s}=-m\psi_0$, $\psi_{-1}=-m\psi_{L_s-1}$:
\begin{equation}
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Pm&0&m\Pp\\-\Pp&0&-\Pm&0\\0&-\Pp&0&-\Pm\\m\Pm&0&-\Pp&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
$\gamma_5$ commutes with $P_\pm$, so $\Gfive Y\Gfive=RYR$, whose entry $(s,s')$ is
$Y_{L_s-1-s,\,L_s-1-s'}$: the matrix rotated by $180^\circ$. $Y\dg$ has the entry
$(Y_{s',s})\dg$, the transpose with each $P_\pm$ unchanged ($P_\pm\dg=P_\pm$, $m$ real). Both give
\begin{equation}
RYR=Y\dg=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Pp&0&m\Pm\\-\Pm&0&-\Pp&0\\0&-\Pm&0&-\Pp\\m\Pp&0&-\Pm&0\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so $\Gfive Y\Gfive=Y\dg$.

\paragraph{$W$ and $Y$ do not commute.} $K$ contains $\gamma_\mu$, and
$\gamma_\mu\Pp=\Pm\gamma_\mu$, so a hop of $K$ exchanges the two projectors of $Y$: $[Y,W]\neq0$.
This one fact makes the M\"obius case different from Shamir's.

\paragraph{Consequence: $\Gfive D\Gfive\neq D\dg$.}
From the three rules above and $D=D_+-D_-Y$,
\begin{align}
\Gfive D\Gfive&=\Gfive D_+\Gfive-\Gfive D_-\Gfive\,\Gfive Y\Gfive=D_+\dg-D_-\dg Y\dg,\nonumber\\
D\dg&=D_+\dg-(D_-Y)\dg=D_+\dg-Y\dg D_-\dg .
\end{align}
The order of the last product differs. Subtracting,
\begin{align}
\Gfive D\Gfive-D\dg&=Y\dg D_-\dg-D_-\dg Y\dg\nonumber\\
&=Y\dg(cW\dg-1)-(cW\dg-1)Y\dg\nonumber\\
&=c\,\big(Y\dg W\dg-W\dg Y\dg\big)=c\,[Y\dg,W\dg],
\end{align}
which is of order $c$ (zero for Shamir, $0.15$ relative on a $12^4$ stout configuration at
$c=0.5$).


\paragraph{The exact relation.}
$D_+$ and $D_-$ are functions of $W$, so they commute. Factor $D_-$ out to the left (formally):
\begin{equation}
D=D_-K_D,\qquad K_D=D_-^{-1}D_+-Y .
\end{equation}
$K_D$ is $\Gfive$-Hermitian:
\begin{align}
\Gfive K_D\Gfive&=\Gfive D_-^{-1}\Gfive\,\Gfive D_+\Gfive-\Gfive Y\Gfive\nonumber\\
&=(D_-\dg)^{-1}D_+\dg-Y\dg\nonumber\\
&=D_+\dg(D_-\dg)^{-1}-Y\dg\qquad(D_\pm\dg\text{ commute})\nonumber\\
&=(D_-^{-1}D_+)\dg-Y\dg=K_D\dg .
\end{align}
So
\begin{align}
D\dg&=K_D\dg D_-\dg\nonumber\\
&=\Gfive K_D\Gfive\,D_-\dg\nonumber\\
&=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,\Gfive\,D_-\dg ,
\end{align}
and multiplying by $D_-\Gfive$ from the left,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;D_-\,\Gfive\,D\dg=D\,\Gfive\,D_-\dg\;}
\end{equation}
with no inverse left. $D_-$ itself cannot be inverted in practice: $D_-=c(W-1/c)$ is $c$ times a
Wilson operator of mass $-M_5-1/c$ ($-3$ for $M_5=1$, $c=0.5$), whose spectrum surrounds the
origin (Sec.~\ref{sec:dminus}) (GCR stalls at
$|r|^2/|b|^2\approx0.74$).


\subsection{The site-diagonal part $C$ and its inverse (note Sec.~II.B.3)}
\label{sec:Cinv}
\paragraph{Where $C^{-1}$ is used.} $C^{-1}$ and $(C\dg)^{-1}$ enter almost every step of the
method:
\begin{itemize}
\item the Pauli--Villars preconditioned operator $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$ and the
substitution $\psi=C^{-1}y$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:Aprime});
\item the Schur complement $S=\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur}), so every
application of $S$ contains one $\Co^{-1}$; one application of $\AR=(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg(S\Ce^{-1})$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pvforms}) contains four site inversions, $\Co^{-1}$, $\Ce^{-1}$,
$(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}$ in $\Spv\dg$ and $(\Cpve^{-1})\dg$;
\item the reconstruction $\psi_e=\Ce^{-1}y$, $\psi_o=\Co^{-1}(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e)$;
\item the two lifts $s$ and $\hat t$ of the prolongation, whose odd parts are built with
$\Co^{-1}$ and with $(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:exact});
\item the solve with $D\dg$, through $(\Ce\dg)^{-1}$ and $(\Co\dg)^{-1}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger});
\item in HMC, $\det\Ce\det\Co$ is a constant and drops out of the probability of the links.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Why it must be exact and cheap.} If $C^{-1}$ needed an iterative solve, each
application of $S$ would contain an inner solve, and the inner tolerance would enter every
Krylov step and every cycle. The derivation below shows that $C^{-1}$ has a closed form: it is
exact, costs $O(L_s)$ per site, is the same matrix on every site, and does not depend on the
gauge field. These four properties are what the uses above assume.

\paragraph{The blocks.} $Y$ maps $\Pp\psi$ to $\Pp\psi$ and $\Pm\psi$ to $\Pm\psi$, so $C$ splits into a block on
$\psi^+=\Pp\psi$ and one on $\psi^-=\Pm\psi$. On $\psi^+$, $Y$ brings $\psi^+_{s-1}$ to slice $s$
with the factor $-1$, and across the wall the factor is $-(-m)=+m$:
\begin{equation}
(C\psi)^+_s=\begin{cases}
d_+\psi^+_0+m\,d_-\psi^+_{L_s-1}, & s=0,\\[2pt]
d_+\psi^+_s-d_-\psi^+_{s-1}, & s\ge1 .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
To solve $C^+x=r$, take the rows $s\ge1$ first:
\begin{equation}
d_+x_s-d_-x_{s-1}=r_s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_s=\frac{r_s+d_-x_{s-1}}{d_+}.
\end{equation}
Each $x_s$ is then affine in the unknown $x_0$. With $q=d_-/d_+$ we claim
\begin{equation}
x_s=p_s+q^sx_0,\qquad p_0=0,\qquad p_s=\frac{r_s+d_-p_{s-1}}{d_+}.
\end{equation}
Check by induction:
\begin{align}
x_s&=\frac{r_s+d_-x_{s-1}}{d_+}\nonumber\\
&=\frac{r_s+d_-(p_{s-1}+q^{s-1}x_0)}{d_+}\nonumber\\
&=\frac{r_s+d_-p_{s-1}}{d_+}+\frac{d_-}{d_+}q^{s-1}x_0\nonumber\\
&=p_s+q^sx_0 .
\end{align}
The remaining row $s=0$ fixes $x_0$:
\begin{align}
d_+x_0+m\,d_-x_{L_s-1}&=r_0\nonumber\\
d_+x_0+m\,d_-\big(p_{L_s-1}+q^{L_s-1}x_0\big)&=r_0\nonumber\\
\big(d_++m\,d_-q^{L_s-1}\big)x_0&=r_0-m\,d_-p_{L_s-1}\nonumber\\
x_0&=\frac{r_0-m\,d_-p_{L_s-1}}{d_++m\,d_-q^{L_s-1}} .
\end{align}
The block on $\psi^-$ is the same with $s\to L_s-1-s$. So $C^{-1}$ costs $O(L_s)$ per site, is
exact, and does not depend on the gauge field. $C\dg$ has the transposed blocks and is inverted
the same way from the other end.


\subsection{The Pauli--Villars preconditioned operator (note Sec.~II.B.4)}
\label{sec:Aprime}
Kanamori, Chen and Matsufuru multiply $D\psi=\eta$ by $(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg$ from the left and set
$\psi=C^{-1}y$:
\begin{align}
D\psi&=\eta\nonumber\\
DC^{-1}y&=\eta\nonumber\\
(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}y&=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg\eta\nonumber\\
A'y&=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg\eta,\qquad A'\equiv(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}.
\end{align}
\paragraph{At $m=1$ it is Hermitian positive.} Then $D=\Dpv$ and $C=\Cpv$, so with
$X=\Dpv\Cpv^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
A'=X\dg X,\qquad z\dg A'z=\|Xz\|^2>0\ \ (z\neq0,\ X\ \text{invertible}).
\end{equation}
\paragraph{At $m<1$ it is a perturbation of that.} $m$ enters $D$ only in the two corners of the
matrix in $s$ ($-mD_-\Pp$ and $-mD_-\Pm$), and $C$ only in its corner $m\,d_-$:
\begin{equation}
D-\Dpv=(1-m)\,D_-\,\big(\Pp\ \text{in corner }(0,L_s-1)+\Pm\ \text{in corner }(L_s-1,0)\big),
\end{equation}
a term localized at the walls. So $A'$ is $X\dg X$ plus a wall term; its eigenvalues stay in the
right half-plane (note Fig.~2), which is what a Krylov method and a polynomial smoother need.

\subsection{Neff's $\alpha$ (note Sec.~II.B.6)}
\label{sec:neff}
Neff's operator is $D^{(\alpha)}=DQ$ with the site-local
\begin{equation}
Q=\operatorname{diag}_s\big(\Pm+\alpha\Pp,\;\alpha,\;\dots,\;\alpha,\;\Pp+\alpha\Pm\big),
\end{equation}
one entry per slice $s=0,\dots,L_s-1$, each acting on the spin.
$Q$ is Hermitian and $Q^{-1}$ is the same with $\alpha\to1/\alpha$. If $D^{(\alpha)}\psi^{(\alpha)}=\eta$, then
\begin{equation}
\eta=D^{(\alpha)}\psi^{(\alpha)}=DQ\psi^{(\alpha)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\psi=Q\psi^{(\alpha)}
\ \text{solves}\ D\psi=\eta .
\end{equation}
$Q$ leaves $\Pm\psi_0$ and $\Pp\psi_{L_s-1}$ alone, so the quark propagator is unchanged.

\section{Even-odd forms (note Sec.~II.C)}

\subsection{Blocks}
\label{sec:eoblocks}
Order the sites even first. $K$ connects nearest neighbors, which have opposite parity, so in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dsplit} the diagonal blocks come from $C$ alone:
\begin{equation}
D=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce & D_{eo}\\ D_{oe} & \Co\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D_{pq}=-\tfrac12K_{pq}(b-cY)\quad(p\neq q).
\end{equation}
$\Ce$ and $\Co$ are the same matrix $C$ on the even and the odd sites.

\subsection{The Schur complement}
\label{sec:schur}
Solve $D\psi=\eta$ block by block. The odd rows:
\begin{align}
D_{oe}\psi_e+\Co\psi_o&=\eta_o\nonumber\\
\psi_o&=\Co^{-1}\big(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e\big).
\label{eq:psio}
\end{align}
Insert into the even rows:
\begin{align}
\Ce\psi_e+D_{eo}\psi_o&=\eta_e\nonumber\\
\Ce\psi_e+D_{eo}\Co^{-1}\big(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e\big)&=\eta_e\nonumber\\
\big(\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\big)\psi_e&=\eta_e-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}\eta_o\nonumber\\
S\psi_e&=\eta'_e,\qquad S\equiv\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe},\quad \eta'_e\equiv\eta_e-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}\eta_o .
\label{eq:schur}
\end{align}

\subsection{$S^{-1}=\pi D^{-1}\iota$}
\label{sec:Sinv}
Let $\iota$ put an even-site field on the whole lattice (zero on the odd sites) and $\pi$ take
the even part:
\begin{equation}
\iota y=\begin{pmatrix}y\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\pi\begin{pmatrix}\psi_e\\\psi_o\end{pmatrix}=\psi_e .
\end{equation}
For $\eta=\iota\eta_e$ we have $\eta_o=0$, so $\eta'_e=\eta_e$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur} reads
$S\psi_e=\eta_e$. Hence
\begin{equation}
S^{-1}\eta_e=\psi_e=\pi D^{-1}\iota\eta_e\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S^{-1}=\pi D^{-1}\iota .
\label{eq:Sinv}
\end{equation}
The same for $D\dg$: its block form is $\begin{pmatrix}\Ce\dg & D_{oe}\dg\\ D_{eo}\dg & \Co\dg\end{pmatrix}$,
and elimination gives $\Ce\dg-D_{oe}\dg(\Co\dg)^{-1}D_{eo}\dg=S\dg$, so $S^{-\dagger}=\pi D^{-\dagger}\iota$.

\subsection{The Pauli--Villars preconditioned forms}
\label{sec:pvforms}
$S$ is not suitable for a Krylov method: its spectrum surrounds the origin. Following Kanamori,
Chen and Matsufuru we multiply from the left by the adjoint of the Pauli--Villars factor and
divide the site part out. Two placements of $\Ce$ give two forms:
\begin{align}
\AR&=(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\,(S\Ce^{-1}),\nonumber\\
\AL&=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,(\Ce^{-1}S).
\end{align}
For $m=1$ both become $X\dg X$, Hermitian positive; for $m<1$ they are perturbations of it, with
all eigenvalues in the right half-plane.

\paragraph{Right form.} Start from $S\psi_e=\eta'_e$ and substitute $\psi_e=\Ce^{-1}y$:
\begin{align}
S\Ce^{-1}y&=\eta'_e\nonumber\\
(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg S\Ce^{-1}y&=(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\eta'_e\nonumber\\
\AR\,y&=\rho_R,\qquad \rho_R\equiv(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\eta'_e,\nonumber\\
\psi_e&=\Ce^{-1}y,\qquad \psi_o=\Co^{-1}(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e).
\end{align}

\paragraph{Left form with $\alpha$ in $D$ only.} For $DQ$ the blocks are $\Ce Q_e$, $D_{eo}Q_o$,
$D_{oe}Q_e$, $\Co Q_o$, and the Schur complement is
\begin{align}
S^{(\alpha)}&=\Ce Q_e-D_{eo}Q_o(\Co Q_o)^{-1}D_{oe}Q_e\nonumber\\
&=\Ce Q_e-D_{eo}Q_oQ_o^{-1}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}Q_e\nonumber\\
&=\big(\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\big)Q_e\nonumber\\
&=SQ_e .
\end{align}
So the left Schur factor of $DQ$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}=(\Ce Q_e)^{-1}SQ_e=Q_e^{-1}\Ce^{-1}SQ_e=Q_e^{-1}\mathcal KQ_e,\qquad \mathcal K\equiv\Ce^{-1}S,
\label{eq:Ksim}
\end{equation}
and the left form becomes $\AL^{(\alpha)}=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg Q_e^{-1}(\Ce^{-1}S)Q_e$. In the right
form the two $Q_e$ cancel: $SQ_e(\Ce Q_e)^{-1}=S\Ce^{-1}$, so $\AR$ does not see $\alpha$.

\section{The factorized coarse-grid correction and the prolongation (note Sec.~III.A)}

\subsection{The factorized coarse correction}
\label{sec:factor}
\paragraph{The inverse of the right form.} Invert the product one factor at a time:
\begin{align}
\AR^{-1}&=\big[(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg(S\Ce^{-1})\big]^{-1}\nonumber\\
&=(S\Ce^{-1})^{-1}\,\big[(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\big]^{-1}\nonumber\\
&=\Ce S^{-1}\,\big[(\Cpve^{-1})\dg\Spv\dg\big]^{-1}\nonumber\\
&=\Ce S^{-1}\,(\Spv\dg)^{-1}\Cpve\dg\nonumber\\
&=\Ce\;\pi D^{-1}\iota\;\;\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota\;\;\Cpve\dg ,
\label{eq:ARinv}
\end{align}
where Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sinv} was used twice. The inverse is a product of the inverses of two
\emph{one-hop} operators on the whole lattice, $D$ and $\Dpv\dg$, and site-local factors.

\paragraph{Galerkin replacement.} With a prolongation $\Pro$ (coarse to whole lattice), replace
each inverse by its Galerkin approximation:
\begin{equation}
X^{-1}\approx\Pro\big(\Pro\dg X\Pro\big)^{-1}\Pro\dg .
\end{equation}
Both coarse operators $\Dc=\Pro\dg D\Pro$ and $\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro$ are nearest-neighbor on the
coarse lattice, because $D$ is one hop and every aggregate contains the whole fifth dimension.

\paragraph{Why the Galerkin approximation is exact on $\operatorname{range}\Pro$.} For any $X$
with $\Pro\dg X\Pro$ invertible and any coarse vector $c$,
\begin{equation}
\Pro\big(\Pro\dg X\Pro\big)^{-1}\Pro\dg X\,(\Pro c)=\Pro\big(\Pro\dg X\Pro\big)^{-1}\big(\Pro\dg X\Pro\big)c=\Pro c .
\end{equation}
So the approximation of $X^{-1}$ inverts $X$ exactly on vectors $X\Pro c$. This is used in the
exactness proof below.

\paragraph{The size of the coarse stencil.} An operator that reaches $k$ hops on the fine lattice
connects a fine site to sites at most $k$ hops away. With blocks at least two sites wide, two coarse
sites can then be coupled only if their coordinates differ by at most $1$ in at most $k$ of the four
directions. Counting the coarse neighbors that differ in exactly $j$ directions ($\binom4j$ choices
of directions, $2^j$ signs):
\begin{align}
k=1:&\quad 1+\tbinom41 2=1+8=9,\nonumber\\
k=2:&\quad 1+8+\tbinom42 2^2=1+8+24=33,\nonumber\\
k\ge4:&\quad \sum_{j=0}^{4}\tbinom4j2^j=3^4=81 .
\end{align}
$D$ and $\Dpv$ are one hop: 9 points each. $\AR$ reaches four hops ($S$ is two hops, and $\AR$
contains $S$ and $\Spv\dg$): 81 points. That is why the correction factorizes instead of
coarsening $\AR$ directly.

The correction of a residual $r$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ARinv} read from the right) is
\begin{equation}
e=O_L\,\pi\Pro\,\Dc^{-1}\Pro\dg\iota\;O_M\;\pi\Pro\big(\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro\big)^{-1}\Pro\dg\iota\;O_R\,r
\end{equation}
with the site-local factors
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
form & $O_R$ & $O_M$ & $O_L$\\
\midrule
right, any $\alpha$ & $\Cpve\dg$ & $1$ & $\Ce$\\
left, $\alpha$ in $D$ only & $1$ & $\Ce Q_e\Cpve\dg$ & $Q_e^{-1}$\\
left, $\alpha$ in $D$ and $\Dpv$ & $Q_e^{-1}$ & $\Ce Q_e^2\Cpve\dg$ & $Q_e^{-1}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In words: (i) put $O_Rr$ on the whole lattice, restrict, solve with the adjoint Pauli--Villars
coarse operator, prolong; (ii) take the even part, multiply by $O_M$, put it back on the whole
lattice, restrict, solve with the quark coarse operator, prolong; (iii) take the even part and
multiply by $O_L$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:correction}). The coarse operators are always those of the plain
$D$ and $\Dpv$, so one setup serves both forms and every $\alpha$.

\paragraph{The left form with $\alpha$.} For $\alpha$ in $D$ only,
\begin{align}
\AL^{(\alpha)\,-1}&=\big[(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,Q_e^{-1}\Ce^{-1}SQ_e\big]^{-1}\nonumber\\

&=Q_e^{-1}S^{-1}\Ce Q_e\,\big[\Spv\dg(\Cpve^{-1})\dg\big]^{-1}\nonumber\\
&=Q_e^{-1}S^{-1}\Ce Q_e\,\Cpve\dg\,(\Spv\dg)^{-1}\nonumber\\
&=\underbrace{Q_e^{-1}}_{O_L}\;\pi D^{-1}\iota\;\underbrace{\Ce Q_e\Cpve\dg}_{O_M}\;
\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota\;\underbrace{1}_{O_R} .
\end{align}
Unlike the right form, the Galerkin factor of $\Dpv\dg$ acts on the plain residual, and
$\Cpve\dg$ lands between the two coarse solves, in $O_M$.

\subsection{Aggregation and the chirality split}
\label{sec:agg}
The fine lattice is divided into $4^4$ blocks, each with the whole fifth dimension; each block is
one coarse site. Every test vector $v$ is split by $\Gfive=\gamma_5R$, $(R\psi)_s=\psi_{L_s-1-s}$:
\begin{equation}
v_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\Gfive)v,\qquad \Gfive v_\pm=\pm v_\pm .
\end{equation}
With $n_v$ test vectors a coarse site has $2n_v$ components, the $+$ parts first. Each column of
$\Pro$ is then an eigenvector of $\Gfive$, so with $\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf 1_{n_v},-\mathbf
1_{n_v})$ per coarse site
\begin{equation}
\Gfive\Pro=\Pro\Sigma .
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Half the storage.} Write the condition $\Gfive v_\pm=\pm v_\pm$ out slice by slice:
\begin{align}
(\Gfive v_\pm)_s&=\gamma_5\,(Rv_\pm)_s=\gamma_5\,v_\pm(L_s-1-s)\nonumber\\
\pm v_\pm(s)&=\gamma_5\,v_\pm(L_s-1-s)\nonumber\\
v_\pm(L_s-1-s)&=\pm\gamma_5\,v_\pm(s) .
\end{align}
So each part is fixed by its slices $s<L_s/2$; $\Pro$ is stored only there (``folded''), in half
precision. Restriction and prolongation unfold on the fly: for the restriction of a fine field $f$,
\begin{equation}
\big(\Pro\dg f\big)_{X,k}=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{s<L_s/2}\Pro_{x,s;k}^{\,*}\,
\big(f_s\pm\gamma_5f_{L_s-1-s}\big)
\end{equation}
(the sign that of the chirality of column $k$), which is what \code{fold\_restrict\_tiled\_kernel}
computes.

\subsection{Orthonormalization per block: CholeskyQR2}
In each block and chirality the columns $w_1,\dots,w_n$ ($n=2\times$ the number of filtered
vectors, see below) are made orthonormal. Collect them as the columns of $W$ and write the QR
decomposition with a positive diagonal of $R$:
\begin{align}
W&=QR\nonumber\\
W\dg W&=R\dg Q\dg QR=R\dg R\nonumber\\
G\equiv W\dg W&=R\dg R\quad\text{(Cholesky)}\nonumber\\
Q&=WR^{-1} .
\end{align}
So one Gram matrix ($n\times n$), one Cholesky factor and one triangular solve give $Q$; the
factorization is unique, so this $Q$ equals what modified Gram--Schmidt produces. In floating
point one pass loses orthogonality like the square of the condition number of $W$, so the pass
is done twice (CholeskyQR2): the second $G$ is close to the identity and repairs the first. Each
pass reads $W$ twice, against one sweep per pair of columns for Gram--Schmidt (800 sweeps for
$n=40$). Where a pivot of the first pass falls below $10^{-12}$ of its diagonal (nearly dependent
columns), the code falls back to Gram--Schmidt for those blocks.

\subsection{The basis $\{s,\hat t\}$ and why the correction is exact}
\label{sec:exact}
The setup produces vectors $y_j$ on the even sites (next section). Each is lifted to the whole
lattice in two ways, one for each coarse solve:
\begin{equation}
s=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce^{-1}y\\-\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat t=\begin{pmatrix}a\\-(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}D_{\mathrm{PV},eo}\dg a\end{pmatrix},\qquad a=\pi Ds ,
\end{equation}
with the even sites on top, as in the block form of $D$ below.
\paragraph{$Ds$ vanishes on the odd sites.}
\begin{align}
Ds&=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce&D_{eo}\\D_{oe}&\Co\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\Ce^{-1}y\\-\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y\end{pmatrix}\nonumber\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce\Ce^{-1}y-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y\\
D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y-\Co\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y\end{pmatrix}\nonumber\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}\big(\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\big)\Ce^{-1}y\\0\end{pmatrix}\nonumber\\
&=\iota\,S\Ce^{-1}y .
\end{align}
In the same way $\Dpv\dg\hat t=\iota\Spv\dg a$.
\paragraph{Exactness of the right form.} Let $s,\hat t\in\operatorname{range}\Pro$ and solve both
coarse systems exactly. The Galerkin map $\Pro(\Pro\dg X\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg X$ is the identity on
$\operatorname{range}\Pro$. Follow $r=\AR y$ through the correction ($O_R=\Cpve\dg$, $O_M=1$,
$O_L=\Ce$):
\begin{align}
O_Rr&=\Cpve\dg(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg S\Ce^{-1}y\nonumber\\
&=\Cpve\dg(\Cpve^{-1})\dg\Spv\dg S\Ce^{-1}y\nonumber\\
&=\Spv\dg\,a,\qquad (a=S\Ce^{-1}y=\pi Ds)\nonumber\\
\iota O_Rr&=\iota\Spv\dg a=\Dpv\dg\hat t\nonumber\\
\Pro\big(\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro\big)^{-1}\Pro\dg\,\Dpv\dg\hat t&=\hat t
\qquad(\hat t\in\operatorname{range}\Pro)\nonumber\\
\pi\hat t&=a=\pi Ds\nonumber\\
\iota O_M\pi\hat t&=\iota\pi Ds=Ds\qquad(Ds\ \text{is zero on the odd sites})\nonumber\\
\Pro\Dc^{-1}\Pro\dg Ds&=s\qquad(s\in\operatorname{range}\Pro)\nonumber\\
e=O_L\pi s&=\Ce\Ce^{-1}y=y .
\end{align}
So the correction inverts $\AR$ exactly on the span of the $y_j$. In practice $\Pro$ is in half
precision and the coarse solves are inexact (relative residual $10^{-1}$), and the left form is
not exact at all; the cycle converges nonetheless.


\paragraph{Why the left form is not exact.} Repeat the argument for $\AL$ with $\alpha=1$ and
$r=\AL y'$, $y'=\Ce^{-1}y$ ($O_R=1$, $O_M=\Ce\Cpve\dg$, $O_L=1$):
\begin{align}
r&=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\Ce^{-1}S\,\Ce^{-1}y=\Spv\dg(\Cpve^{-1})\dg\Ce^{-1}a,\qquad a=S\Ce^{-1}y,\nonumber\\
\iota O_Rr&=\iota\Spv\dg u,\qquad u\equiv(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}\Ce^{-1}a .
\end{align}
The first solve returns the Pauli--Villars lift of $u$, not of $a$. $u$ differs from $a$ by the
site-local matrix $(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}\Ce^{-1}$, which mixes the slices $s$ and the spin, so its lift
is in general not in the span of the $\hat t_j$ made from $a$. The correction is then only
approximate. In practice the left form converges within a few iterations of the right form (note
Sec.~V), so the cycle does not need the exactness.

\subsection{The coarse operators by probing}
\label{sec:probe}
Write $\phi_{X,k}$ for column $k$ ($k=1,\dots,2n_v$) of $\Pro$ on block $X$: it equals the
orthonormalized vector on the sites of $X$ and vanishes outside $X$. By the two previous
subsections
\begin{equation}
\langle\phi_{X,j}|\phi_{Y,k}\rangle=\delta_{XY}\delta_{jk},\qquad
\Pro=\sum_{X,k}|\phi_{X,k}\rangle\langle X,k| ,
\end{equation}
where $|X,k\rangle$ is the unit vector of component $k$ on coarse site $X$. The coarse operator
$\Dc=\Pro\dg D\Pro$ (the ``little Dirac operator'' of L\"uscher's deflation) and the coarse
correction are then
\begin{equation}
(\Dc)_{jk}(X,Y)=\langle\phi_{X,j}|D|\phi_{Y,k}\rangle,\qquad
\Pro\Dc^{-1}\Pro\dg=\sum_{X,Y,j,k}|\phi_{X,j}\rangle\,(\Dc^{-1})_{jk}(X,Y)\,\langle\phi_{Y,k}| ,
\end{equation}
and in the same way for $\Dpv\dg$. $D$ hops only to nearest neighbors in four dimensions and a
block holds the whole fifth dimension, so $(\Dc)(X,Y)$ vanishes unless $Y=X$ or $Y=X\pm\hat\mu$.
Each coarse site $X$ therefore has a $2n_v\times2n_v$ diagonal block $A(X)=\Dc(X,X)$ and one link
block $B_\mu^\pm(X)=\Dc(X,X\pm\hat\mu)$ per direction: nine blocks. They are found column by column. For component $k$,
put a unit vector $\epsilon_k$ on \emph{every} coarse site and prolong:
$\phi=\Pro\epsilon_k$. Split $D=C+H$ ($H$ the hops) and $H$ by leg (direction and sign), and split
each leg into the part that stays inside its block and the part that crosses to the neighbor
block:
\begin{align}
D\phi&=C\phi+\sum_{\text{legs }l}H_l\phi\nonumber\\
&=\underbrace{C\phi+\sum_lH_l^{\rm in}\phi}_{\text{stays in }X}
+\sum_l\underbrace{H_l^{\rm cross}\phi}_{\text{comes from }X\pm\hat\mu} .
\end{align}
Restricting, $\big(\Pro\dg(C\phi+\sum_lH^{\rm in}_l\phi)\big)_{X}$ is column $k$ of $A(X)$, and
$\big(\Pro\dg H^{\rm cross}_l\phi\big)_X$ is column $k$ of the link block of leg $l$, since every
coarse site carries the same unit vector and a crossing hop brings exactly the neighbor's
contribution. Doing this for all $2n_v$ components fills all nine blocks (\code{coarse\_blocks},
\code{hop\_split}, \code{restrict\_leg}). Several components are probed at a time, as many as a
quarter of the free device memory allows (four on $24^4$), so that $\Pro$ is read once for them.

\section{The coarse solvers (note Sec.~III.B)}
\label{sec:coarsesolve}
\paragraph{Quark factor.} $\Dc c=g$ by BiCGStab on its even-odd Schur complement, preconditioned by
the inverse diagonal blocks. With $\Dc=\begin{pmatrix}A_{ee}&B_{eo}\\B_{oe}&A_{oo}\end{pmatrix}$ on
the coarse lattice, exactly as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:schur},
\begin{align}
\big(A_{ee}-B_{eo}A_{oo}^{-1}B_{oe}\big)c_e&=g_e-B_{eo}A_{oo}^{-1}g_o,\nonumber\\
c_o&=A_{oo}^{-1}\big(g_o-B_{oe}c_e\big).
\end{align}
\paragraph{Pauli--Villars factor.} $Xw=g$ with $X=\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro=(\Dcpv)\dg$. The code stores
$X$ itself (the adjoint of the probed $\Dcpv$, \code{CoarseOp::adjoint}) and a second copy $\Dcpv=X\dg$,
and solves the Tikhonov-regularized normal equation by CG on all coarse sites, without a preconditioner:
\begin{equation}
\big(X\dg X+\tau\big)\,w=X\dg g,\qquad \tau=10^{-3}.
\end{equation}
To see what the shift does, write the singular value decomposition $X=\sum_k\sigma_ku_kv_k\dg$ ($\sigma_k>0$,
the $u_k$ and the $v_k$ orthonormal). The exact solution is $w=\sum_k\sigma_k^{-1}(u_k\dg g)\,v_k$; the
regularized one is
\begin{equation}
w_\tau=\sum_k\frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_k^2+\tau}\,(u_k\dg g)\,v_k
=\sum_k\frac{\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_k^2+\tau}\,\frac{u_k\dg g}{\sigma_k}\,v_k ,
\end{equation}
so each component is multiplied by $\sigma_k^2/(\sigma_k^2+\tau)$: close to $1$ for
$\sigma_k\gg\sqrt\tau$, close to $\sigma_k^2/\tau$ for $\sigma_k\ll\sqrt\tau$. With stout smearing
the Galerkin factor has singular values far below those of the fine $\Dpv$ (note Sec.~V.C.3); the
exact solve would amplify those directions by $1/\sigma_k$, and the shift caps the amplification at
$1/(2\sqrt\tau)$. CG on $X\dg X+\tau$ needs one application of $X$ and one of $X\dg$ per iteration.
Both factors stop at $|r|^2/|b|^2<10^{-2}$ (for the Pauli--Villars factor, the residual of the normal
equation).

\paragraph{Levels below.} At small masses the coarse solves themselves become expensive (on
$24^4$ at $m=0.001$ about 2000 applications of $\Dc$ per correction). The coarse sites are then
grouped into super-blocks (2, then 3), local vectors are formed from $\Pro\dg s_j$, $\Pro\dg\hat
t_j$, and a V-cycle preconditions the coarse solve of the quark factor. Number the levels from the
coarse lattice, $\ell=1$, down; let $\mathcal D_\ell$ be the operator on level $\ell$, $B_\ell$ its
diagonal blocks, and $\Pro_{\ell+1}$ the prolongation from level $\ell+1$ to level $\ell$ ($Q_{\ell+1}$ in the note). The
preconditioner $M_\ell$ on level $\ell$ is defined from the one below:
\begin{align}
z&=\Pro_{\ell+1}\,M_{\ell+1}\,\Pro_{\ell+1}\dg\,r &&\text{(restrict, precondition one level down, prolong)}\nonumber\\
M_\ell\,r&=z+B_\ell^{-1}\big(r-\mathcal D_\ell z\big) &&\text{(one block-Jacobi step on the residual)}
\end{align}
and on the last level $M_\ell=\mathcal D_\ell^{-1}$, inverted densely. This brings the coarse cost down to about 50 applications per
correction.

\section{Setup (note Sec.~III.C)}
The setup produces the vectors $y_j$, then $\{s_j,\hat t_j\}$, $\Pro$, the coarse operators and the
levels. It always uses the right form.

\subsection{Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration}
\label{sec:filter}
The $y_j$ should approximate the low right singular vectors of $\AR$, i.e.\ the low eigenvectors of
$H_n=\AR\dg\AR$. With $\ell(x)=\big(2x-(\lambda_{\rm hi}+\lambda_{\rm lo})\big)/(\lambda_{\rm
hi}-\lambda_{\rm lo})$, the polynomial $T_d(\ell(x))$ is bounded by $1$ on
$[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ and grows fast below $\lambda_{\rm lo}$. Collect the $n_f$ vectors as the columns of $\mathcal Y=(y_1,\dots,y_{n_f})$ ($X$ in the note's
Algorithm~1). One round:
\begin{align}
\mathcal Y&\leftarrow T_d\big(\ell(H_n)\big)\mathcal Y &&\text{(recurrence below)}\nonumber\\
\mathcal Y&\leftarrow\text{orthonormalize }\mathcal Y\nonumber\\
G&=\mathcal Y\dg H_n\mathcal Y=V\Theta V\dg,\quad \mathcal Y\leftarrow\mathcal YV &&\text{(Rayleigh--Ritz)}\nonumber\\
\lambda_{\rm lo}&\leftarrow\theta_{\max} .
\end{align}
$G$ is the $n_f\times n_f$ matrix of $H_n$ in the span of $\mathcal Y$; its eigenvalues
$\theta_1\le\dots\le\theta_{\max}$ (the Ritz values) estimate the $n_f$ lowest eigenvalues of
$H_n$, and $\mathcal YV$ the eigenvectors. Setting $\lambda_{\rm lo}=\theta_{\max}$ makes the
next round damp everything above the span already found and amplify what lies below it. The
filter is applied without the coefficients of the polynomial. With $L=\ell(H_n)$,
\begin{equation}
T_0(L)\mathcal Y=\mathcal Y,\qquad T_1(L)\mathcal Y=L\mathcal Y,\qquad
T_{k+1}(L)\mathcal Y=2L\,T_k(L)\mathcal Y-T_{k-1}(L)\mathcal Y ,
\end{equation}
so $T_d(L)\mathcal Y$ costs $d$ applications of $H_n$ per column.

\paragraph{How much the filter amplifies, and the degree.} For $|t|\ge1$,
$|T_d(t)|=\cosh\!\big(d\operatorname{arccosh}|t|\big)$. A mode with $x$ just below $\lambda_{\rm lo}$
has $\ell(x)=-(1+\delta)$ with small $\delta>0$, and
\begin{align}
\operatorname{arccosh}(1+\delta)&=\ln\!\Big(1+\delta+\sqrt{2\delta+\delta^2}\Big)\approx\sqrt{2\delta},\nonumber\\
|T_d(\ell(x))|&\approx\cosh\!\big(d\sqrt{2\delta}\big).
\end{align}
The lowest modes have $x\approx0$, where
\begin{equation}
\ell(0)=-\frac{\lambda_{\rm hi}+\lambda_{\rm lo}}{\lambda_{\rm hi}-\lambda_{\rm lo}}
\approx-\Big(1+2\frac{\lambda_{\rm lo}}{\lambda_{\rm hi}}\Big),\qquad
\delta\approx2\frac{\lambda_{\rm lo}}{\lambda_{\rm hi}},\qquad
|T_d(\ell(0))|\approx\cosh\!\Big(2d\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm lo}/\lambda_{\rm hi}}\Big),
\end{equation}
while every mode inside $[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ stays below $1$. With the code's
degree $d=1.5\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm hi}/\lambda_{\rm lo}}$ the lowest modes gain a factor
$\cosh3\approx10$ per round over the damped ones: the degree is chosen for a fixed amplification
per round.

In the code the degree is $d=1.5\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm hi}/\lambda_{\rm lo}}$, between 40 and 1000; the first round
uses $\lambda_{\rm lo}=\lambda_{\rm hi}/1000$. \emph{Observation} ($24^4$, $m=0.001$, 20 vectors): after
the first round (degree 48) the largest Ritz value is still $0.34\,\lambda_{\rm hi}$, so the
second round has degree 40 and damps only the top two thirds of the spectrum. The filter is weak;
the bootstrap does most of the work.

\subsection{Bootstrap}
Each round builds $\Pro$ and the cycle $\mathcal M$ from the current $y$, replaces every $y_j$ by four
steps of FGMRES on $\AR x=y_j$ from $x=0$ preconditioned by $\mathcal M$, and orthonormalizes $y$
through its Gram matrix. To see why this works, write $y_j$ in eigenvectors of the preconditioned operator. The
cycle already handles the components inside its coarse space, and FGMRES with it removes them
first; what remains, and is therefore amplified relative to the rest, is what the coarse space
misses. In HMC the levels below are used from the second round on (in the first, on the filtered
vectors, the coarse solves were as fast without them).

\subsection{Cost and memory}
On $24^4$ ($L_s=8$, 20 filtered vectors, $m=0.001$; the $24^3\times48$ runs of the note use
16, $n=32$): the filter about 35\,s and the bootstrap
about 77\,s, the setup in all about 113\,s. A bootstrap round makes its correction ($\Pro$ 1.8\,s,
the two coarse operators about 4.4\,s each, the inverse blocks 0.8\,s, the top of the smoother
1.3\,s; the levels 7\,s more in the second round) and then improves the 20 vectors (about 20\,s in
the first round, 26\,s in the second). $\Pro$ ($40$ columns, half precision, whole lattice) is the largest
object, about 4.7\,GB.

\section{The Chebyshev smoother and the cycle (note Sec.~III.D)}
\label{sec:cycle}
One cycle $y=\mathcal Mb$ is
\begin{align}
y&\leftarrow p(\tilde A)\,b &&\text{(pre-smoothing)}\nonumber\\
r&\leftarrow b-\tilde Ay\nonumber\\
y&\leftarrow y+e(r) &&\text{(coarse correction)}\nonumber\\
r&\leftarrow b-\tilde Ay\nonumber\\
y&\leftarrow y+p(\tilde A)\,r &&\text{(post-smoothing)}.
\end{align}


\paragraph{Cost of one cycle.} Count the applications of $\tilde A$: the pre-smoothing ($\nu$
Chebyshev steps from $x=0$) costs $\nu-1$, the residual after it $1$, the residual after the
correction $1$, the post-smoothing $\nu-1$:
\begin{equation}
(\nu-1)+1+1+(\nu-1)=2\nu ,
\end{equation}
plus the two coarse solves. One application of $\tilde A$ is two Schur factors, each two hops:
$\nu=8$ means 16 applications, $64$ applications of $D_{eo}$ or $D_{oe}$, per cycle. This is why one multigrid iteration costs as
much as about 24 iterations of CG on $\mathcal K\dg \mathcal K$ (two hops each with $\mathcal K$ and $\mathcal K\dg$).

\subsection{The Chebyshev smoother, derived}
The smoother is $\nu$ steps of Chebyshev iteration on $\tilde Ax=b$ from $x=0$. Choose the interval
$[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ that it damps ($\lambda_{\rm hi}$ slightly above the spectral
radius from a power iteration, $\lambda_{\rm lo}=\lambda_{\rm hi}/\kappa_s$) and set
\begin{equation}
\theta=\tfrac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}+\lambda_{\rm lo}),\qquad
\delta=\tfrac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}-\lambda_{\rm lo}),\qquad \sigma=\theta/\delta .
\end{equation}
The residual after $k$ steps is $r_k=R_k(\tilde A)r_0$ with the scaled Chebyshev polynomial
\begin{equation}
R_k(\lambda)=\frac{T_k\big((\theta-\lambda)/\delta\big)}{T_k(\sigma)},\qquad R_k(0)=1,
\end{equation}
which is at most $1/T_k(\sigma)$ in modulus on the interval. With $\ell$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:filter} built on this interval, $(\theta-\lambda)/\delta=-\ell(\lambda)$ and
$\sigma=-\ell(0)$, so $R_k(\lambda)=T_k(\ell(\lambda))/T_k(\ell(0))$: the polynomial of the setup
filter, normalized to $1$ at the origin. The filter applies it to $H_n$ to amplify the modes below
$\lambda_{\rm lo}$; the smoother applies it to $\tilde A$ to damp the modes in
$[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ and leaves the low modes to the coarse correction. Write $t=(\theta-\lambda)/\delta$ and
use $T_{k+1}(t)=2tT_k(t)-T_{k-1}(t)$:
\begin{align}
T_{k+1}(\sigma)R_{k+1}&=2t\,T_k(\sigma)R_k-T_{k-1}(\sigma)R_{k-1}\nonumber\\
R_{k+1}&=\rho_k\big[2t\,R_k-\rho_{k-1}R_{k-1}\big],\qquad \rho_k\equiv\frac{T_k(\sigma)}{T_{k+1}(\sigma)} .
\end{align}
The same recurrence at $t=\sigma$ gives the $\rho$'s:
\begin{align}
T_{k+1}(\sigma)&=2\sigma T_k(\sigma)-T_{k-1}(\sigma)\nonumber\\
\frac1{\rho_k}&=2\sigma-\rho_{k-1},\qquad \rho_0=\frac{T_0(\sigma)}{T_1(\sigma)}=\frac1\sigma .
\end{align}
Now split $2t=2\sigma-2\lambda/\delta$ and use $2\sigma=1/\rho_k+\rho_{k-1}$:
\begin{align}
R_{k+1}&=\rho_k\Big[\big(2\sigma-\tfrac{2\lambda}{\delta}\big)R_k-\rho_{k-1}R_{k-1}\Big]\nonumber\\
&=\rho_k\Big[\big(\tfrac1{\rho_k}+\rho_{k-1}\big)R_k-\tfrac{2\lambda}{\delta}R_k-\rho_{k-1}R_{k-1}\Big]\nonumber\\
&=R_k+\rho_k\rho_{k-1}\big(R_k-R_{k-1}\big)-\frac{2\rho_k}{\delta}\lambda R_k .
\end{align}
Apply to $r_0$, with $\lambda\to\tilde A$:
\begin{equation}
r_{k+1}-r_k=\rho_k\rho_{k-1}(r_k-r_{k-1})-\frac{2\rho_k}{\delta}\tilde Ar_k .
\end{equation}
Since $r=b-\tilde Ax$, a step $x_{k+1}=x_k+d_k$ changes the residual by $-\tilde Ad_k$. Matching
the two sides,
\begin{equation}
d_k=\rho_k\rho_{k-1}\,d_{k-1}+\frac{2\rho_k}{\delta}\,r_k .
\end{equation}
The first step: $R_1=T_1(t)/T_1(\sigma)=t/\sigma=1-\lambda/\theta$, so $x_1=b/\theta$ and
$d_0=r_0/\theta$. These are the updates of the code (\code{BlasChebyshevStep}): $\nu$ steps cost
$\nu-1$ applications of $\tilde A$, and the polynomial is fixed, so the smoother is a linear map.
The runs use $\nu=8$, $\kappa_s=10$ with stout links ($M_5=1$) and $\nu=12$, $\kappa_s=30$ without
($M_5=1.8$).

\section{The outer solver: flexible GCR (note Sec.~III.E)}
\label{sec:gcr}
The outer iteration solves $\tilde Ay=\rho$ ($\tilde A=\AR$ or $\AL$) with a right preconditioner
$\mathcal M$, the multigrid cycle. Because $\mathcal M$ contains Krylov solves stopped by a
residual test, it changes from one application to the next, so the method must be flexible: it
keeps the preconditioned directions $z_i$ themselves.

One step of GCR($k$), from the residual $|r_i\rangle=|\rho\rangle-\tilde A|y_i\rangle$, with $j$
running over the last $k$ kept pairs:
\begin{align}
|z_i\rangle&=\mathcal M|r_i\rangle &&\text{(one cycle)}\nonumber\\
|q_i\rangle&=\tilde A|z_i\rangle &&\text{(one application of }\tilde A)\nonumber\\
\gamma_{ij}&=\frac{\langle q_j|q_i\rangle}{\langle q_j|q_j\rangle},\quad
|q_i\rangle\leftarrow|q_i\rangle-\sum_j\gamma_{ij}|q_j\rangle,\quad
|z_i\rangle\leftarrow|z_i\rangle-\sum_j\gamma_{ij}|z_j\rangle\nonumber\\
\beta_i&=\frac{\langle q_i|r_i\rangle}{\langle q_i|q_i\rangle}\nonumber\\
|y_{i+1}\rangle&=|y_i\rangle+\beta_i|z_i\rangle,\qquad |r_{i+1}\rangle=|r_i\rangle-\beta_i|q_i\rangle .
\end{align}
The third line applies the projector $1-\sum_j|q_j\rangle\langle q_j|/\langle q_j|q_j\rangle$ to
$|q_i\rangle$, so the kept $|q_j\rangle$ are mutually orthogonal, and the same combination of the
$|z_j\rangle$ keeps $\tilde A|z_i\rangle=|q_i\rangle$ true. The step length $\beta_i$ ($\gamma$ and $\beta$ as in the note's Algorithm~4, which also normalizes
each $|q_i\rangle$) minimizes
$\big\||r_i\rangle-\beta|q_i\rangle\big\|$: it removes from $|r_i\rangle$ its component along
$|q_i\rangle$, and $|r_{i+1}\rangle=|\rho\rangle-\tilde A|y_{i+1}\rangle$ holds exactly. With $k=2$
(GCR(2)) the code keeps $k+1=3$ pairs $(z,q)$ and the residual, seven fields on the even sites, at
the price of no optimality over the whole Krylov space.

\section{Solving with $D\dg$ (note Sec.~IV)}
\label{sec:dagger}
HMC needs $(D\dg D)^{-1}$, so it solves with $D\dg$ as often as with $D$. A second setup for $D\dg$
would double the cost. The tool that avoids it is an exact relation between $D$ and $D\dg$.

The $\Gfive$-transformation and the exact relation it rests on were derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:g5}.

\subsection{$\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ as a preconditioner of $D\dg$ (note Sec.~IV.A)}
\label{sec:c4}
Multiply $D\dg$ from the right by $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ and use the exact relation of Sec.~\ref{sec:g5} in the form
$D\dg=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,\Gfive D_-\dg$, together with $\Gfive D_-\dg\Gfive=D_-$:
\begin{align}
D\dg\,\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive&=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,\Gfive D_-\dg\,\Gfive\,D^{-1}\Gfive\nonumber\\
&=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,D_-\,D^{-1}\,\Gfive\nonumber\\
&=\Gfive D_-^{-1}\big(D_-D+[D,D_-]\big)D^{-1}\Gfive\nonumber\\
&=\Gfive\big(1+D_-^{-1}[D,D_-]D^{-1}\big)\Gfive .
\end{align}
The commutator, with $D=D_+-D_-Y$ and $[D_+,D_-]=0$:
\begin{align}
[D,D_-]&=[D_+,D_-]-[D_-Y,D_-]\nonumber\\
&=-\big(D_-YD_--D_-D_-Y\big)=-D_-[Y,D_-]\nonumber\\
&=-D_-[Y,cW-1]=-c\,D_-[Y,W] .
\end{align}
The factor $D_-$ cancels against $D_-^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;D\dg\,\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive=\Gfive\,(1-N)\,\Gfive,\qquad N=c\,[Y,W]\,D^{-1}\;}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{The commutator written out.} Split the hop into its spin-scalar part and its
$\gamma$ part, $K=K_0-K_\gamma$ with
\begin{align}
(K_0\psi)(x)&=\sum_\mu\big[U_\mu(x)\psi(x+\hat\mu)+U_\mu\dg(x-\hat\mu)\psi(x-\hat\mu)\big],\nonumber\\
(K_\gamma\psi)(x)&=\sum_\mu\gamma_\mu\big[U_\mu(x)\psi(x+\hat\mu)-U_\mu\dg(x-\hat\mu)\psi(x-\hat\mu)\big].
\end{align}
$K_0$ commutes with $Y$. $K_\gamma$ does not: $\gamma_\mu P_\mp=P_\pm\gamma_\mu$, so
$K_\gamma P_\mp=P_\pm K_\gamma$. Slice by slice,
\begin{align}
(YK_\gamma\psi)_s&=-\Pm K_\gamma\psi_{s+1}-\Pp K_\gamma\psi_{s-1},\nonumber\\
(K_\gamma Y\psi)_s&=-\Pp K_\gamma\psi_{s+1}-\Pm K_\gamma\psi_{s-1},\nonumber\\
([Y,K_\gamma]\psi)_s&=(\Pp-\Pm)K_\gamma\big(\psi_{s+1}-\psi_{s-1}\big)=\gamma_5K_\gamma\big(\psi_{s+1}-\psi_{s-1}\big),
\end{align}
with the wrap-around of $Y$. The constant part of $W$ commutes with $Y$, so
$[Y,W]=-\frac12[Y,K]=\frac12[Y,K_\gamma]$:
\begin{equation}
\big([Y,W]\psi\big)_s=\tfrac12\,\gamma_5K_\gamma\big(\psi_{s+1}-\psi_{s-1}\big).
\label{eq:YWcomm}
\end{equation}
It is a difference in $s$ followed by one hop that flips chirality ($\gamma_5$ keeps it,
$\gamma_\mu$ flips it). It is bounded, so $N$ is large only through $D^{-1}$.

\paragraph{$N^2$ written out.} Expand $D^{-1}$ in the right and left eigenvectors of $D$
(assumed diagonalizable), $D|u_k\rangle=\lambda_k|u_k\rangle$, $\langle w_k|D=\lambda_k\langle w_k|$,
$\langle w_k|u_l\rangle=\delta_{kl}$:
\begin{align}
D^{-1}&=\sum_k\frac{|u_k\rangle\langle w_k|}{\lambda_k},\nonumber\\
N&=c\sum_k\frac{[Y,W]|u_k\rangle\,\langle w_k|}{\lambda_k},\nonumber\\
N^2&=c^2\sum_{k,l}\frac{[Y,W]|u_k\rangle\;\langle w_k|[Y,W]|u_l\rangle\;\langle w_l|}{\lambda_k\lambda_l} .
\end{align}
$N$ is large through the terms of small $\lambda_k$, one per low mode: a large part of low rank,
which GCR removes in about as many extra steps as there are such modes. $N^2$ would be large
through the terms where both $\lambda_k$ and $\lambda_l$ are small, and each of them carries the
matrix element $\langle w_k|[Y,W]|u_l\rangle$ between two low modes. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:YWcomm}
this matrix element is small: a low mode carries $\Pm$ at the wall $s=0$ and $\Pp$ at
$s=L_s-1$, so $[Y,W]|u_l\rangle$ carries the opposite chirality on each wall, and its overlap with
another low mode comes only from the bulk of the fifth dimension, where the low modes have
decayed. The left eigenvectors are localized in the same way, since $\Gfive D\Gfive=D\dg$ up to
$O(c)$. So $N^2$ is small to the extent that
\begin{equation}
c\,\big|\langle w_k|[Y,W]|u_l\rangle\big|\ll|\lambda_k|\qquad\text{for the low modes }k,l,
\end{equation}
and then $(1-N)^{-1}=1+N+N^2+\dots\approx1+N$: GCR needs only a few more steps than for the
identity. This is an argument, not a bound: the matrix elements have not been computed, and the
iteration counts are the test. On $16^4$ it needs 2--3 iterations more than the solve with $D$; on $12^4$ in setup~S (stout links with $\rho=0.1$ and three steps, $M_5=1$; note Table~1), GCR(2) stalls on the residual right-hand side of HMC (note, Sec.~V.B.2).

\subsection{On the even sites (note Sec.~IV.B)}
The solves of HMC are on the even sites. Since $\Gfive$ acts within a site it commutes with $\pi$
and $\iota$, and with Eq.~\eqref{eq:Sinv}
\begin{equation}
S^{-\dagger}=\pi D^{-\dagger}\iota\approx\pi\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive\iota=\Gfive\,\pi D^{-1}\iota\,\Gfive
=\Gfive S^{-1}\Gfive .
\end{equation}
The solve of $D\dg w=r$:
\begin{align}
r'_e&=r_e-D_{oe}\dg(\Co\dg)^{-1}r_o &&\text{so that }S\dg w_e=r'_e,\nonumber\\
w_e&=(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z &&\Longrightarrow\ S\dg(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z=r'_e\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathcal K\dg z=r'_e,\nonumber\\
z&=\text{GCR on }\mathcal K\dg\text{ with preconditioner }\Ce\dg\,\Gfive S^{-1}\Gfive,\nonumber\\
S^{-1}t&\approx\Ce^{-1}\,\mathcal M\,(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg t &&\text{(one cycle of }\AR),\nonumber\\
w_e&=(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z,\qquad w_o=(\Co\dg)^{-1}\big(r_o-D_{eo}\dg w_e\big).
\end{align}
The line $\mathcal K\dg z=r'_e$ uses $\mathcal K=\Ce^{-1}S$: $\mathcal K\dg=S\dg(\Ce\dg)^{-1}$. The approximation for $S^{-1}$
comes from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ARinv}: $S^{-1}=\Ce^{-1}\AR^{-1}(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg$. In the
preconditioner, $(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg t$ is the right-hand side of $\AR$ for the source $(t,0)$ (no
odd part), so it is applied directly as one adjoint Schur factor of $\Dpv$. Each GCR step costs
one cycle, as for $D$.

\subsection{Why $D_-$ cannot be inverted (note Sec.~IV.C)}
\label{sec:dminus}
The exact relation of Sec.~\ref{sec:g5} contains $D_-$, and one could try to solve with $D\dg$
through $D$ exactly by inverting $D_-$. Rewrite it:
\begin{equation}
D_-=cW-1=c\Big(W-\frac1c\Big),
\end{equation}
$c$ times a Wilson operator of mass $-M_5-1/c$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:wfree} with $M_5\to M_5+1/c$, its
free eigenvalues are
\begin{equation}
c\Big[-M_5-\frac1c+\sum_\mu(1-\cos p_\mu)\pm i\Big(\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu\Big)^{1/2}\Big].
\end{equation}
For $M_5=1$, $c=0.5$ the real part of the bracket runs from $-3$ to $5$; near real part $0$ the
imaginary parts have both signs. The spectrum surrounds the origin. A Krylov method needs a
polynomial $p$ with $p(0)=1$ that is small on the spectrum, and on a set around the origin no
low-degree polynomial does that (by the maximum principle $|p|$ on a curve around $0$ is at least
$|p(0)|=1$). GCR(8) on $D_-$ indeed stops at $|r|^2/|b|^2\approx0.74$ after 20000 iterations.
The preconditioner of Sec.~\ref{sec:c4} never inverts $D_-$.

\section{Why the method converges (note Sec.~V.D)}
\label{sec:why}
The outer solver works on $\mathcal A\equiv\tilde A\mathcal M$, the operator times the cycle. The
note asks which property of $\mathcal A$ explains the convergence it measures. The tools below
need $\mathcal A$ to be one fixed linear map, so in this section the coarse solves are exact; with
Krylov coarse solves stopped at $10^{-2}$ the cycle changes from one application to the next.

\subsection{The field of values and the bound for GCR}
\paragraph{Definition.} The field of values of a matrix $\mathcal A$ is the set of Rayleigh
quotients,
\begin{equation}
W(\mathcal A)=\Big\{\frac{\langle z|\mathcal A|z\rangle}{\langle z|z\rangle}\;:\;|z\rangle\neq0\Big\}.
\end{equation}
It contains the spectrum: for an eigenvector, $\mathcal A|x\rangle=\lambda|x\rangle$ gives
$\langle x|\mathcal A|x\rangle/\langle x|x\rangle=\lambda$. Its leftmost point is set by the Hermitian
part $\mathcal H=\frac12(\mathcal A+\mathcal A\dg)$. Since
$\langle z|\mathcal A\dg|z\rangle=\langle z|\mathcal A|z\rangle^*$,
\begin{equation}
\Re\langle z|\mathcal A|z\rangle=\tfrac12\big(\langle z|\mathcal A|z\rangle+\langle z|\mathcal A|z\rangle^*\big)
=\langle z|\mathcal H|z\rangle\ \ge\ \lambda_{\min}(\mathcal H)\,\langle z|z\rangle ,
\end{equation}
so $\min\Re W(\mathcal A)=\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal H)$.

\paragraph{One step of GCR.} Take the residual $|r\rangle$ and the new direction $\mathcal M|r\rangle$,
so $|q\rangle=\tilde A\mathcal M|r\rangle=\mathcal A|r\rangle$. Minimizing over $\beta$ alone,
\begin{equation}
\min_\beta\big\||r\rangle-\beta|q\rangle\big\|^2=\langle r|r\rangle-\frac{|\langle q|r\rangle|^2}{\langle q|q\rangle}
\le\langle r|r\rangle-\frac{\big(\Re\langle r|\mathcal A|r\rangle\big)^2}{\|\mathcal A\|^2\langle r|r\rangle},
\end{equation}
using $|\langle q|r\rangle|\ge|\Re\langle r|\mathcal A|r\rangle|$ and $\langle q|q\rangle\le\|\mathcal A\|^2\langle r|r\rangle$.
If $W(\mathcal A)$ lies in the right half-plane, $c\equiv\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal H)>0$ and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_{\rm new}\|^2}{\|r\|^2}\le1-\frac{c^2}{\|\mathcal A\|^2}<1 .
\end{equation}
GCR($k$) does at least as well: its residual is orthogonal to the kept $|q_j\rangle$, so it
minimizes over their span together with $\mathcal A|r\rangle$, which contains the one-direction
choice above. This is the bound of Eisenstat, Elman and Schultz: every step reduces the residual,
for every $k$, including $k=0$.

\paragraph{When $W$ contains the origin.} Then some $|r\rangle$ has $\langle r|\mathcal A|r\rangle=0$.
If such an $|r\rangle$ is reached and the kept directions do not help, $\langle q|r\rangle$ can vanish,
so $\beta=0$, the residual does not change, and with a fixed cycle the next step repeats the same
direction. The bound gives nothing, and convergence has to come from elsewhere. The note finds
that the coarse correction pushes $W$ across the origin, and that the spectrum stays away from it.

\subsection{The spectrum and eigenvalue condition numbers}
The spectrum lies inside $W$, so it can stay far from the origin while $W$ does not. For a
non-normal $\mathcal A$ the eigenvalues alone do not bound the residual: the eigenvectors are not
orthogonal. Let $|x\rangle$ and $\langle y|$ be the normalized right and left eigenvectors of
$\lambda$. The spectral projector onto $\lambda$ is
\begin{equation}
P_\lambda=\frac{|x\rangle\langle y|}{\langle y|x\rangle},\qquad
\|P_\lambda\|=\frac{\|x\|\,\|y\|}{|\langle y|x\rangle|}=\frac1{|\langle y|x\rangle|}\equiv\kappa_\lambda ,
\end{equation}
the condition number of $\lambda$. It is $1$ when $|x\rangle$ and $|y\rangle$ coincide (a normal matrix)
and large when they are nearly orthogonal.

\subsection{Pseudospectra and the bound for GMRES}
\paragraph{Definition.} The $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum is the set of $z$ where
$\sigma_{\min}(z-\mathcal A)<\varepsilon$, i.e.\ where $\|(z-\mathcal A)^{-1}\|>1/\varepsilon$. For a
normal matrix it is the $\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the spectrum; for a non-normal one it can be
much larger.

\paragraph{The bound.} Full GMRES picks the best polynomial with $p_k(0)=1$:
$|r_k\rangle=p_k(\mathcal A)|r_0\rangle$ with $\|r_k\|$ minimal, so for any such polynomial
$\|r_k\|/\|r_0\|\le\|p_k(\mathcal A)\|$. Let a contour $\Gamma$ of length $L$ enclose the spectrum
but not the origin, with $\sigma_{\min}(z-\mathcal A)\ge\varepsilon$ on $\Gamma$. By Cauchy's formula
for a matrix,
\begin{align}
p_k(\mathcal A)&=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma p_k(z)\,(z-\mathcal A)^{-1}\,dz,\nonumber\\
\|p_k(\mathcal A)\|&\le\frac1{2\pi}\,L\,\max_{z\in\Gamma}|p_k(z)|\,\max_{z\in\Gamma}\|(z-\mathcal A)^{-1}\|
\le\frac{L}{2\pi\varepsilon}\max_{z\in\Gamma}|p_k(z)| .
\end{align}
\paragraph{A circle.} Take $\Gamma$ the circle $|z-c|=r$ with $r<c$ (it excludes the origin) and
$p_k(z)=(1-z/c)^k$. On the circle $|1-z/c|=|c-z|/c=r/c$ and $L=2\pi r$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\frac r\varepsilon\Big(\frac rc\Big)^k :
\end{equation}
convergence at the rate $r/c$ per iteration, with the constant $r/\varepsilon$ set by how far the
pseudospectrum reaches.

\paragraph{An outlier.} An eigenvalue $\lambda_o$ outside the circle is removed by a factor of the
polynomial, $p_k(z)=(1-z/\lambda_o)(1-z/c)^{k-1}$, which vanishes at $\lambda_o$. For a simple
$\lambda_o$,
$p_k(\mathcal A)P_o=p_k(\lambda_o)P_o=0$, so $p_k(\mathcal A)=p_k(\mathcal A)(I-P_o)$, and the
integral needs only the part of the spectrum inside the circle:
\begin{align}
\|(z-\mathcal A)^{-1}(I-P_o)\|&\le\|(z-\mathcal A)^{-1}\|\,\|I-P_o\|\le\frac{\kappa_o}{\varepsilon},\nonumber\\
|1-z/\lambda_o|&=\frac{|\lambda_o-z|}{|\lambda_o|}\le\frac{|\lambda_o-c|+r}{|\lambda_o|}\qquad(|z-c|=r),\nonumber\\
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}&\le\kappa_o\,\frac r\varepsilon\,\frac{|\lambda_o-c|+r}{|\lambda_o|}\Big(\frac rc\Big)^{k-1},
\end{align}
using $\|I-P_o\|=\|P_o\|=\kappa_o$ for an oblique projector. The outlier costs one iteration and a
constant factor, not the rate.

\paragraph{Checking a contour with finitely many points.} $\sigma_{\min}$ is $1$-Lipschitz:
$|\sigma_{\min}(z-\mathcal A)-\sigma_{\min}(z'-\mathcal A)|\le\|(z-z')I\|=|z-z'|$. If it is at least
$\varepsilon_0$ at points spaced by $h$ on $\Gamma$, it is at least $\varepsilon_0-h/2$ everywhere on
$\Gamma$. This is why the note can state the bound on its dense circles and only an estimate on the
contours with 16 points.

\subsection{Truncated GCR with inexact coarse solves}
The bounds above are for a fixed $\mathcal M$ and, for the pseudospectra, for full GMRES. The
solver runs GCR(2) with coarse solves to $10^{-2}$, so $\mathcal M$ changes from step to step and
neither bound applies directly. The note checks this case numerically (Sec.~V.D.4). The one
mechanism of failure that a fixed cycle allows is the repeated direction of the previous
subsection, a plateau of the residual.
