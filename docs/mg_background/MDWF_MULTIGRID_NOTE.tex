\title{Multigrid for the even-odd preconditioned M\"obius domain-wall operator\\
with a factorized Pauli--Villars coarse correction}
\author{Wei-Lun Chen\\[0.3ex]{\small\href{mailto:wlchenba@gmail.com}{\texttt{wlchenba@gmail.com}}}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Domain-wall fermions preserve chiral symmetry up to residual violations that decay with the
fifth-dimensional extent $L_s$, but solving the M\"obius domain-wall Dirac equation suffers from
critical slowing down toward the physical light-quark mass. Kanamori, Chen, and Matsufuru showed
that preconditioning with the Pauli--Villars operator and removing the site-diagonal part yields
$(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$, whose eigenvalues all lie in the right half-plane with a compressed
upper end, so that non-Hermitian Krylov solvers apply. Earlier four-dimensional Pauli--Villars
multigrid methods, however, did not outperform even-odd conjugate gradient (CG), as Boyle and
Yamaguchi found.

In this work, we construct a two-grid method for the even-odd preconditioned form of this operator
in which the quark and Pauli--Villars factors are coarsened separately. Each aggregate spans the
entire fifth dimension, yielding a four-dimensional coarse lattice with nearest-neighbor couplings,
and additional coarse levels below the first replace explicit deflation. These choices address the
\New three points drawn from the comparison of Boyle and Yamaguchi. A single setup applies to every
value of Neff's parameter $\alpha$, and \New we show that the multigrid hierarchy built for $D$ can
also be reused for solves with $D\dg$, as required in hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) simulations, without
a second setup.
\ifresults{} Across four quenched $24^3\times48$ configurations, the iteration
count increases by a factor of $2.0$--$2.4$ between $m=0.1$ and $m=0.001$, compared with $20$--$32$
for mixed-precision even-odd CGNE \New without deflation. At $m=0.001$, a single
solve is $6.4$--$10.9$ times faster than mixed-precision CGNE, and a complete 12-column
point-source propagator takes \New $0.27$--$0.49$ of its wall-clock time
including setup.\else{} We state the formulation and outline the remaining open questions.\fi{}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many lattice computations need fermions whose chiral symmetry is broken only slightly at finite
lattice spacing. Operators of different chirality then do not mix, which weak matrix elements
require, as in the Standard Model calculation of $K\to\pi\pi$ decays and
$\varepsilon'/\varepsilon$~\cite{RBCKpipi}. \New Other calculations use
such fermions as well, including the hadronic vacuum polarization and light-by-light contributions to the
muon $g-2$~\cite{RBCHVP,RBCWindow,RBCHLbL}, the $B\to\pi\ell\nu$ form factors on fine lattices
with the heavy quark in the same M\"obius domain-wall action~\cite{JLQCDBpi}, inclusive
semileptonic $B$ decays~\cite{HashimotoInclusive,GambinoInclusive,BaroneInclusive}, and parton
distribution amplitudes~\cite{BakerPionDA}. The overlap operator~\cite{NarayananNeuberger,Neuberger}
is exactly chiral on the lattice. Domain-wall fermions~\cite{Kaplan,Shamir,FurmanShamir} cost less.
They add a fifth dimension of extent $L_s$, in which a large negative Wilson mass $-M_5$ binds modes
of opposite chirality to its two boundaries, and the residual breaking falls as $L_s$ grows. The
M\"obius generalization~\cite{BNO0,BNO1,BNO} adds two coefficients $(b,c)$ that reduce the residual
breaking at fixed $L_s$; $c=0$ is Shamir's operator.

Inverting the domain-wall operator is expensive, because the matrix is neither Hermitian nor
positive definite and each application requires $L_s$ four-dimensional Wilson hopping operations.
In practice, the standard solver remains the conjugate gradient method~\cite{HestenesStiefel} on
the normal equations of the even-odd preconditioned operator (CGNE), which squares the condition
number. As the quark mass $m$ decreases, the smallest eigenvalues of $D$ approach $m$, so the
condition number of the normal equations grows as $1/m^2$ and the CG iteration count as $1/m$ until
cut off by the finite lattice volume (critical slowing down). For Wilson-type fermions, adaptive
aggregation multigrid~\cite{Brannick,Babich,OsbornClover,DDalphaAMG,ClarkQUDA,BridgeMG} has
eliminated this slowing down by using the local coherence of the low eigenmodes~\cite{Luscher}. A
small set of block-restricted near-null vectors spans the low-mode subspace and keeps the solver
cost nearly independent of the quark mass.

\paragraph{Multigrid for domain-wall fermions.}
For domain-wall fermions, multigrid is harder. Because of the large negative Wilson mass $-M_5$,
the spectrum of $D$ encloses the origin, so non-Hermitian Krylov methods converge only at degrees
comparable to the matrix dimension~\cite{NRT,BoyleMRHS}. Boyle~\cite{BoyleMRHS} summarizes four
approaches, distinguished by the operator chosen for coarsening:
\begin{itemize}
\item Cohen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Cohen} coarsened the squared operator using blocks that cover
the entire fifth dimension, giving a two-hop coarse operator.
\item HDCG~\cite{HDCG} accelerates CGNE by coarsening the squared even-odd Schur complement, with
an $81$-point coarse stencil for blocks of at least $4^4$.
\item HDCR~\cite{HDCR} coarsens the Hermitian indefinite operator $\gamma_5RD$ at $c=0$, where $R$
reflects the fifth dimension. Its coarse operator stays nearest-neighbor, which keeps the setup
cost manageable for HMC.
\item Brower, Clark, Howarth, and Weinberg~\cite{BCHW} noted that the Pauli--Villars preconditioned
operator $\Dpv^{-1}D$ isolates the physical four-dimensional degrees of freedom, and solved
$\Dpv\dg Dx=\Dpv\dg b$ on all lattice sites. Projecting the Wilson kernel slice by slice gives
nearest-neighbor coarse operators $\hat D$ and $\hat D_{\rm PV}$, and the coarse-grid correction
solves $\hat D_{\rm PV}\dg\hat D\,\hat e=\hat r$. They tested Shamir fermions in the
two-dimensional Schwinger model.
\end{itemize}
Boyle and Yamaguchi~\cite{BY} compared \New HDCR, the Pauli--Villars method of Ref.~\cite{BCHW}, HDCG and hybrids of them for Shamir fermions in four dimensions. On
large volumes, the combined setup and solve time of HDCR fell below that of a single even-odd CG
solve. When they applied the Pauli--Villars method of Ref.~\cite{BCHW} to four-dimensional QCD,
however, \New with $2^4$ blocks it needed roughly half the fine-matrix applications of red-black CG but $18$--$31$ times
its solve time (their Table~VIII). From their discussion we draw three points: (i) the relevant baseline is even-odd (red-black) CG,
against which a full-lattice solve is slow; (ii) keeping a fifth dimension of extent $L_s$ on the
coarse grid makes the coarse solve too costly; and (iii) reducing that cost requires either further
coarse levels or coarse-grid deflation, neither of which was included in their PV implementation.
They also found that CG converged faster on the Schur complement than on its left-preconditioned
form\ifresults{} (for the M\"obius setups of this work at $\alpha=0.6$ the order is reversed; see
Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform})\fi, and that an even-odd smoother did not work with the HDCR coarse
correction.

\paragraph{Recent work by Boyle and collaborators.}
The multiple right-hand-side HDCG of Ref.~\cite{BoyleMRHS} solves the normal equation of the
even-odd operator with a two-level CG whose coarse space is spanned by blocked low modes. Treating
many right-hand sides at once makes the coarse space cheap, with gains of up to twenty over
even-odd CG at physical quark mass. Boyle and Oare~\cite{BoyleOare} coarsen the two-hop operator
$\Dpv\dg D$, a $33$-point stencil, aiming at solves on a new gauge field as in HMC. Its field of
values can reach the left half-plane although its spectrum does not, so the GCR bound of
Eisenstat, Elman and Schultz~\cite{ESS} is lost; they add a shift to the smoothers. On
$48^3\times96$ at the physical quark mass \New they estimate $578$ fine-matrix applications, against
$26500$ for red-black CG.

\paragraph{Setup cost in ensemble generation.}
A multigrid setup is paid once per gauge field, which is affordable for propagators but not for
HMC. Eick and Mawhinney~\cite{EickMawhinney} avoid the setup by blocking a fine M\"obius ensemble
to a coarse lattice by renormalization-group methods. The interpolated correction then carries
high modes that the fine operator amplifies by four orders of magnitude, and they remove them with
a Chebyshev filter of degree $150$--$300$.

\paragraph{The preconditioned operator.}
Kanamori, Chen and Matsufuru~\cite{KCM2024} looked for a preconditioned M\"obius operator local
enough for a multigrid. Approximating $\Dpv$ by its block-diagonal part $\Bpv$ brings back
eigenvalues with negative real part, so the Pauli--Villars factor has to be kept whole. Dividing out
the site-diagonal part $C$ as well gives $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$, whose eigenvalues all lie
in the right half-plane, with the smallest condition number among the operators they studied.
Neff's form of the domain-wall matrix~\cite{Neff,CKMN} rescales the fifth dimension by a site-local
factor $Q$ and changes the conditioning of the even-odd system.

\paragraph{This work.}
We construct an adaptive aggregation multigrid~\cite{Brannick,DDalphaAMG,BridgeMG} for the even-odd
counterpart of $A'$, building on the Pauli--Villars approach of Refs.~\cite{BCHW,BY} while
addressing each of \New these three points. As in standard adaptive aggregation, the
coarse space is built from block-restricted near-null vectors and the coarse operators are Galerkin
projections. The outer solve is formulated on the even-odd system, matching the red-black CG
baseline; its inverse factorizes into the inverses of $D$ and $\Dpv\dg$, each replaced by a
Galerkin coarse inverse. \New We coarsen the quark factor as $DB_5^{-1}$, with the site-local
$B_5=b-cY$, which is $\Gfive$-Hermitian where $D$ is not, so its coarse operator is
$\sigma_3$-Hermitian (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dsymherm}, Sec.~\ref{sec:coarseops}). Every aggregate contains the entire fifth dimension, so the coarse lattice
is four-dimensional and both coarse operators remain nearest-neighbor. To treat the remaining low
modes of the quark factor without explicit coarse-grid deflation, we add further aggregation levels
below the coarse lattice with a dense LU solve at the bottom (Sec.~\ref{sec:coarse}). For the Pauli--Villars coarse
system, we apply CG to a Tikhonov-regularized normal equation to suppress small singular values
created by the Galerkin projection. \New Each even-site setup vector is lifted
to the full lattice in two ways (one for each coarse solve), which makes the coarse-grid correction
exact on the test vectors for the right-preconditioned form (Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact}). The smoother is
a fixed-degree Chebyshev polynomial, and the setup combines Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration
with a bootstrap stage.

Our second result concerns HMC trajectories, which require solves with $D\dg$ as frequently as with
$D$. \New When the M\"obius coefficients $b$ and $c$ are independent of $s$, an
exact similarity relation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:b5}, reduces a solve with $D\dg$ to a single solve with
$D$ using the same multigrid hierarchy. When the coefficients depend on $s$, \New the multigrid cycle of $D$, conjugated by
$\Gfive=\gamma_5R$, can serve as a preconditioner for $D\dg$; Eq.~\eqref{eq:c4} gives its error
for constant $b$ and $c$, and we test it with constant coefficients. Neither route requires a second multigrid setup.\ifresults{} We benchmark the method on
quenched gauge configurations ranging from $12^4$ to $24^3\times48$ on a single GPU against
even-odd CGNE \New without deflation.\fi

\section{The operator and its preconditioning}
\label{sec:opsec}

\subsection{Spectra of preconditioned M\"obius operators}
\label{sec:spectra}

The distribution of the spectrum determines which iterative methods can converge efficiently. A
Krylov solver constructs a residual polynomial $p$ normalized by $p(0)=1$ that is small on the
spectrum; a low-degree polynomial with this property exists only if the spectrum can be enclosed by
a region, such as an ellipse, that excludes the origin. The same requirement applies to the
Chebyshev smoother of Sec.~\ref{sec:smoother}, which is defined on an ellipse in the right
half-plane. Because the eigenvalues of the unpreconditioned domain-wall operator straddle the
origin, preconditioning is required to shift the spectrum into a single half-plane.
Figures~\ref{fig:free} and~\ref{fig:A}, reproduced from Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}, illustrate the
spectrum before and after applying both preconditioners; \New the intermediate preconditioned
operators discussed below are shown in Fig.~3 of Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{spectrum_fig1.png}
\caption{Spectrum of a free M\"obius domain-wall operator on $16^4\times8$ with
quark mass $0.01$, $b=1.5$, $c=0.5$ and domain-wall height $M_5=1$.
From Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}, Fig.~1.}
\label{fig:free}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The unpreconditioned operator.}
The free spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:free}) is a band that reaches into $\Re\lambda<0$, and so does
the spectrum on a $2+1$-flavor configuration at the physical point (\New Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}, Fig.~3). So CG and BiCGStab~\cite{vanderVorst} do not work on $D$ itself, and the practical choice has
been CG on a positive definite squared operator.

\paragraph{Site-diagonal preconditioning.}
The site-diagonal part $C$ has an inverse that is the same matrix on every site
(Sec.~\ref{sec:C}). With it the spectrum of $DC^{-1}$ shrinks, but it still lies on both sides of
the origin.

\paragraph{Pauli--Villars preconditioning.}
The operator $\Dpv^{-1}D$, with $\Dpv=D(m=1)$, approximates the overlap operator~\cite{BCHW}.
Since $\Dpv^{-1}$ costs as much as a solve with $D$, Ref.~\cite{BCHW} used $\Dpv\dg$ instead.
$\Dpv\dg D$ has positive real parts. Its low modes lie close to the imaginary axis,
shifted by about the quark mass, as for an overlap operator. Its high end, however, reaches far
beyond that of an overlap operator.

\paragraph{Both together.}
The operator $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$ has all its eigenvalues in the right half-plane
(Fig.~\ref{fig:A}, left), with the smallest condition number in Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}. With
$\Dpv$ replaced by its block-diagonal approximation $\Bpv$, which drops the hops between $4^4$
blocks, negative real parts come back (right). Our method therefore keeps the factor exact on the
fine lattice and approximates it only through its Galerkin coarse operator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:correction}).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{spectrum_fig4.png}
\caption{Left: spectrum of $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Aprime}, on a JLQCD
$2+1$-flavor configuration at the physical point; all real parts are positive. Right: the same
with $\Dpv$ replaced by its block-diagonal approximation $\Bpv$; negative real parts reappear.
From Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}, Fig.~4.}
\label{fig:A}
\end{figure}

The figures show the full-lattice operator $A'$; the even-odd operators of Sec.~\ref{sec:eo} are not shown.

\subsection{The operator and Pauli--Villars preconditioning}
\label{sec:operator}

The notation
follows Ref.~\cite{BNO} except where stated; Appendix~\ref{sec:notation} relates it to
Ref.~\cite{KCM2024} and to Bridge++ symbol by symbol.

\subsubsection{The M\"obius operator}
\paragraph{Ingredients.} $\psi$ is a field on the four-dimensional lattice with an extra
index $s=0,\dots,L_s-1$, the fifth dimension; $\psi_s$ is an ordinary Dirac field for each
$s$. $P_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\gamma_5)$ are the chiral projectors. The Wilson hopping term is
\begin{equation}
(K\psi)(x)=\sum_{\mu=1}^{4}\Big[(1-\gamma_\mu)U_\mu(x)\psi(x+\hat\mu)
+(1+\gamma_\mu)U_\mu\dg(x-\hat\mu)\psi(x-\hat\mu)\Big],
\end{equation}
and the Wilson operator with mass $-M_5$ (the domain-wall height $M_5>0$) is
\begin{equation}
W=(4-M_5)-\tfrac12K .
\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
This is the usual $D_W(-M_5)$ with hopping coefficient $\tfrac12$. $W$ acts on each slice
$s$ in the same way and does not mix slices. Its free spectrum, needed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}, is $\{-M_5+\sum_\mu(1-\cos p_\mu)\pm i(\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu)^{1/2}\}$.

\paragraph{The operator.} With
\begin{equation}
D_+=1+bW,\qquad D_-=cW-1
\label{eq:Dpm}
\end{equation}
(Ref.~\cite{BNO}, Eq.~(2.18)), the M\"obius operator at quark mass $m$ acts slice by slice as
\begin{equation}
(D\psi)_s=D_+\psi_s+D_-\big(\Pm\psi_{s+1}+\Pp\psi_{s-1}\big),\qquad
\psi_{L_s}\equiv-m\,\psi_0,\quad \psi_{-1}\equiv-m\,\psi_{L_s-1}
\label{eq:Dslice}
\end{equation}
(Ref.~\cite{BNO}, Eq.~(2.20), with $s$ counted from $0$). For $L_s=4$, as a matrix in $s$,
\begin{equation}
D=\begin{pmatrix}
D_+ & D_-\Pm & 0 & -mD_-\Pp\\
D_-\Pp & D_+ & D_-\Pm & 0\\
0 & D_-\Pp & D_+ & D_-\Pm\\
-mD_-\Pm & 0 & D_-\Pp & D_+
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:Dmatrix}
\end{equation}
Shamir's operator is $b=1$, $c=0$; the results of this work use $b=1.5$, $c=0.5$
($b-c=1$, $b+c=2$). The quark mass enters only through the two corners. The physical
four-dimensional quark field is $q=\Pm\psi_0+\Pp\psi_{L_s-1}$.

\paragraph{The hop in the fifth dimension.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dslice} separates into a part
that hops in the fifth dimension and one that does not. Define
\begin{equation}
(Y\psi)_s=-\Pm\psi_{s+1}-\Pp\psi_{s-1}\qquad\text{(with the same wrap-around)}.
\label{eq:Y}
\end{equation}
For $L_s=4$, as a matrix in $s$,
\begin{equation}
Y=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -\Pm & 0 & m\Pp\\
-\Pp & 0 & -\Pm & 0\\
0 & -\Pp & 0 & -\Pm\\
m\Pm & 0 & -\Pp & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:Ymatrix}
\end{equation}
This is the off-diagonal part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dmatrix} with the factor $D_-$ removed and
the sign reversed. Then
$D=D_+-D_-Y$. Here $D_-$ stands to the \emph{left} of $Y$, since in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dslice} it
acts on the shifted and projected field, and this order matters in Sec.~\ref{sec:g5}. Inserting
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:W} and \eqref{eq:Dpm},
\begin{equation}
D=1+bW-(cW-1)Y=1+Y+W\,(b-cY)=C-\tfrac12K\,(b-cY),\qquad C=d_++d_-Y,
\label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
with $d_+=1+b(4-M_5)$ and $d_-=1-c(4-M_5)$. In the last step $W=(4-M_5)-\frac12K$ was split
into its site-local constant and its hop; the constant parts form $C$. Note
$d_-=-(c(4-M_5)-1)$ is \emph{minus} the site-local part of $D_-$, because $Y$ carries the
sign. The Pauli--Villars operator is the same operator at unit mass, $\Dpv=D|_{m=1}$,
$\Cpv=C|_{m=1}$; $D_\pm$ and $K$ do not depend on $m$.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\Gfive$}{Gamma5}-transformation and the exact adjoint}
\label{sec:g5}
For the Wilson operator $\gamma_5W\gamma_5=W\dg$, so a solve with $W\dg$ is a solve with $W$
between two multiplications by $\gamma_5$. The analogue for domain-wall fermions uses
\begin{equation}
\Gfive=\gamma_5R,\qquad (R\psi)_s=\psi_{L_s-1-s} .
\end{equation}
Each building block transforms as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $W$, $D_+$ and $D_-$ act on every slice alike, so they commute with $R$, and
$\gamma_5W\gamma_5=W\dg$. Hence $\Gfive W\Gfive=W\dg$, $\Gfive D_\pm\Gfive=D_\pm\dg$.
\item $Y$ has no hop in the four dimensions, so $\Gfive Y\Gfive$ can be read off
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Y}. $\gamma_5$ commutes with $P_\pm$; $R$ turns the hop $s\to s+1$ into
$s\to s-1$. So $\Gfive Y\Gfive$ has $-\Pm$ on the hop down and $-\Pp$ on the hop up,
and \New the corners $m\Pp$ and $m\Pm$ exchange places. This is the transpose of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Y} in $s$ with $P_\pm$ in place, which is $Y\dg$ since $P_\pm\dg=P_\pm$ and $m$
is real, giving $\Gfive Y\Gfive=Y\dg$.
\item $W$ and $Y$ do not commute, because $\gamma_\mu$ in $K$ anticommutes with $\gamma_5$, so
$\gamma_\mu P_\pm=P_\mp\gamma_\mu$, and the hop of $K$ exchanges the two projectors of $Y$.
\end{enumerate}
From $D=D_+-D_-Y$ it follows that
\begin{equation}
\Gfive D\Gfive=D_+\dg-D_-\dg Y\dg ,\qquad\text{whereas}\qquad D\dg=D_+\dg-Y\dg D_-\dg ,
\label{eq:g5D}
\end{equation}
the adjoint reversing the order of the product. The difference is a commutator,
\begin{equation}
\Gfive D\Gfive-D\dg=Y\dg D_-\dg-D_-\dg Y\dg=c\,[Y\dg,W\dg]=\tfrac{c}{2}\big[K\dg,Y\dg\big],
\label{eq:g5herm}
\end{equation}
where $D_-\dg=cW\dg-1$ and $W\dg=(4-M_5)-\frac12K\dg$ were used. It vanishes for Shamir's
operator ($c=0$) and is of order $c$ otherwise; multigrid on the $\Gfive$-Hermitian operator has been used
in the $c=0$ subspace of M\"obius fermions (as summarized in Ref.~\cite{BoyleMRHS}). Thus $\Gfive D\Gfive\neq D\dg$ for M\"obius
fermions, \New and $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ is not the inverse of $D\dg$. Conjugating the multigrid of
$D$ with $\Gfive$ therefore does not by itself solve with $D\dg$. Sec.~\ref{sec:c4idea} uses it as a
preconditioner.

\paragraph{The exact relation.} It is the generalized $\Gfive$-Hermiticity of
Ref.~\cite{BNO}, Eqs.~(2.22)--(2.23), whose $\Gfive$ contains $D_-^{-1}$; here $\Gfive=\gamma_5R$
and $D_-$ appears explicitly. $D_+$ and $D_-$ are functions of the same $W$ and commute.
Where $D_-$ is invertible, factor it out to the left:
\begin{equation}
D=D_-\,K_{D},\qquad K_{D}=D_-^{-1}D_+-Y .
\end{equation}
$K_D$ is $\Gfive$-Hermitian. By item 1, $\Gfive D_-^{-1}D_+\Gfive=(D_-\dg)^{-1}D_+\dg$, and
this equals $(D_-^{-1}D_+)\dg=D_+\dg(D_-\dg)^{-1}$ because $D_+\dg$ and $D_-\dg$ commute;
with item 2, $\Gfive K_D\Gfive=K_D\dg$. Then
\begin{equation}
D\dg=K_D\dg D_-\dg=\Gfive K_D\Gfive\,D_-\dg=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,\Gfive D_-\dg ,
\end{equation}
and multiplying by $D_-\Gfive$ from the left,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;D_-\,\Gfive\,D\dg=D\,\Gfive\,D_-\dg\;}
\label{eq:g5exact}
\end{equation}
This form has no inverse, holds for any $m$ (so also for $\Dpv$, with the same $D_-$), and can
be checked by applying both sides to a vector. The Hermitian operator
of the M\"obius case is $\Gfive K_D=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D$; Ref.~\cite{BNO} \New defines, after its Eq.~(2.22),
its $\Gamma_5$ as $\gamma_5RD_-^{-1}$ for that reason. It contains $D_-^{-1}$, whose cost
is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger}.

\subsubsection{The site-diagonal part and its inverse}
\label{sec:C}
$C=d_++d_-Y$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:D} is what remains of $D$ when the four-dimensional hops are
dropped. It acts within one four-dimensional site, as an $L_s\times L_s$ matrix in $s$ times
the identity in color, and it commutes with $P_\pm$ (only $Y$ is involved, and $Y$ maps
$\Pp\psi$ to $\Pp\psi$ and $\Pm\psi$ to $\Pm\psi$). So it splits into a block on
$\psi^+=\Pp\psi$ and one on $\psi^-=\Pm\psi$. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:Y}, $Y$ moves $\psi^+$ from
$s-1$ to $s$ and $\psi^-$ from $s+1$ to $s$, with the factor $-1$, and the wrap-around gives
$+m$:
\begin{equation}
(C\psi)^+_s=\begin{cases}d_+\psi^+_0+m\,d_-\psi^+_{L_s-1}, & s=0,\\
d_+\psi^+_s-d_-\psi^+_{s-1}, & s\ge1,\end{cases}
\label{eq:Cblocks}
\end{equation}
a lower bidiagonal matrix with one corner element; for $\psi^-$ the same with
$s\to L_s-1-s$ (upper bidiagonal). For $L_s=4$ the block on $\psi^+$ is
\begin{equation}
C^+=\begin{pmatrix}d_+&0&0&m\,d_-\\-d_-&d_+&0&0\\0&-d_-&d_+&0\\0&0&-d_-&d_+\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The inverse.} To solve $C^+x=r$, write the rows $s\ge1$ as
$x_s=(r_s+d_-x_{s-1})/d_+$. Starting from an unknown $x_0$, each $x_s$ is affine in $x_0$.
With $q=d_-/d_+$,
\begin{equation}
x_s=p_s+q^{s}x_0,\qquad p_0=0,\quad p_s=(r_s+d_-p_{s-1})/d_+ .
\end{equation}
The row $s=0$,
$d_+x_0+m\,d_-x_{L_s-1}=r_0$, with $x_{L_s-1}=p_{L_s-1}+q^{L_s-1}x_0$, fixes $x_0$:
\begin{equation}
x_0=\frac{r_0-m\,d_-\,p_{L_s-1}}{d_++m\,d_-\,q^{L_s-1}},\qquad x_s=p_s+q^{s}x_0 .
\label{eq:Cinv}
\end{equation}
For $C^-$ the substitution runs from $s=L_s-1$ downwards. $C\dg$ has the transposed blocks,
an upper bidiagonal matrix on $\psi^+$ (solved like $C^-$) and a lower one on $\psi^-$. $C^{-1}$
is thus exact, costs $O(L_s)$ per site, and does not depend on the gauge field~\cite{KCM2024}.
It exists unless $d_++m\,d_-q^{L_s-1}=0$.

\paragraph{\New The factor $B_5$.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:D} also reads
\begin{equation}
D=1+Y+W B_5,\qquad B_5=b-cY .
\label{eq:DB5}
\end{equation}
With Eq.~\eqref{eq:Y},
\begin{equation}
(B_5\psi)_s=b\,\psi_s+c\,\big(\Pm\psi_{s+1}+\Pp\psi_{s-1}\big),
\end{equation}
where the boundary terms that wrap around carry the factor $-mc$. For $L_s=4$, as a matrix in $s$
(compare Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ymatrix}),
\begin{equation}
B_5=\begin{pmatrix}
b & c\Pm & 0 & -mc\Pp\\
c\Pp & b & c\Pm & 0\\
0 & c\Pp & b & c\Pm\\
-mc\Pm & 0 & c\Pp & b
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:B5matrix}
\end{equation}
$B_5$ acts within each four-dimensional site and has the form $d_++d_-Y$ of $C$ with $d_+=b$ and
$d_-=-c$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:Cinv} inverts it exactly. Like $C$, it depends on $m$ through the
corners. The properties of Sec.~\ref{sec:g5} give $\Gfive Y\dg\Gfive=Y$ and
$\Gfive W\dg\Gfive=W$, hence $\Gfive B_5\dg\Gfive=B_5$ and $\Gfive D\dg\Gfive=1+Y+B_5W$. Because
$B_5$ is a polynomial in $Y$, it commutes with $1+Y$, so $B_5DB_5^{-1}=1+Y+B_5W$ as well, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\Gfive D\dg\Gfive=B_5\,D\,B_5^{-1}\;}
\label{eq:b5sim}
\end{equation}
Sec.~\ref{sec:correction} coarsens $DB_5^{-1}$ with it, and Sec.~\ref{sec:b5} solves with $D\dg$
through it.

\subsubsection{The preconditioned operator}
Ref.~\cite{KCM2024} multiplies $D\psi=\eta$
from the left by $(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg$ and substitutes $\psi=C^{-1}y$:
\begin{equation}
A'y=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg\,\eta,\qquad A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg\,D\,C^{-1},\qquad \psi=C^{-1}y .
\label{eq:Aprime}
\end{equation}
The two factors are chosen for these reasons:
\begin{itemize}
\item For $m=1$, $DC^{-1}=\Dpv\Cpv^{-1}$ and $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})$, which is
Hermitian and positive definite. For $m<1$ the two factors differ only in the corners of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dmatrix}, and $A'$ is a perturbation of a positive operator.
\item $\Dpv^{-1}D$ approximates the overlap operator~\cite{BCHW}. \New With $\Dpv\dg$, which needs no inverse of $\Dpv$,
one application of $A'$ costs two applications of $D$ plus the
site-local $C^{-1}$ and $\Cpv^{-1}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Link smearing}
\label{sec:smearing}
\New The fermion operator may be built from smeared links; smearing
integrates the surrounding gauge field into each link and suppresses the short-distance
fluctuations that produce near-zero modes of $W$~\cite{LinSmear}. We use the stout projection combined with
the APE staples~\cite{MorningstarPeardon}:
\begin{equation}
C_\mu(x)=\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\rho\Big[U_\nu(x)U_\mu(x+\hat\nu)U_\nu\dg(x+\hat\mu)
+U_\nu\dg(x-\hat\nu)U_\mu(x-\hat\nu)U_\nu(x-\hat\nu+\hat\mu)\Big],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
U'_\mu(x)=\exp\!\Big(\big[C_\mu(x)U_\mu\dg(x)\big]_{\rm AT}\Big)\,U_\mu(x),
\label{eq:stout}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdots]_{\rm AT}$ is the anti-Hermitian traceless part and $\rho$ a tunable parameter.
The step~\eqref{eq:stout} is applied repeatedly; ``stout $\rho\times n$'' means $n$ steps with
the same $\rho$. Only $W$ (through $K$) sees the smeared links; \New $Y$ and $C$ do not depend
on the gauge field.

\subsubsection{Neff's \texorpdfstring{$\alpha$}{alpha}}
Neff's form of the domain-wall matrix~\cite{Neff,CKMN} is $D^{(\alpha)}=DQ$ with a
site-local $Q$, diagonal in the fifth dimension,
\begin{equation}
Q_0=\Pm+\alpha\Pp,\qquad Q_s=\alpha\ \ (0<s<L_s-1),\qquad Q_{L_s-1}=\Pp+\alpha\Pm .
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
The following properties, each read off Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q}, are used below:
\begin{itemize}
\item $Q$ is Hermitian, and $Q^{-1}$ is Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q} with $\alpha\to1/\alpha$.
\item $Q$ leaves the components $\Pm\psi_0$ and $\Pp\psi_{L_s-1}$ unchanged, which make up the
quark field $q$; so $D^{(\alpha)}$ and $D$ have the same four-dimensional propagator.
\item The solution of $D\psi=\eta$ is $\psi=Q\psi^{(\alpha)}$ with
$D^{(\alpha)}\psi^{(\alpha)}=\eta$, since $D^{(\alpha)}\psi^{(\alpha)}=DQ\psi^{(\alpha)}=D\psi$.
\item $Q$ is site-local, so the site-diagonal part of $DQ$ is $CQ$, with
$(CQ)^{-1}=Q^{-1}C^{-1}$ and $(CQ)\dg=QC\dg$.
\end{itemize}
The factor may be applied to the quark operator alone or to the Pauli--Villars operator as
well; we write $\Dpv^{(\alpha)}=\Dpv Q$ in the latter case. For $\alpha<1$ it improves the
conditioning of the even-odd operator~\cite{Neff,CKMN}.

\subsection{Even-odd forms}
\label{sec:eo}

In the even-odd block form the diagonal blocks of $D$ are $C$. Order the sites so that the even ones come first.
We write \New $\mathcal F_{pq}$ for the block of an operator $\mathcal F$ that maps the sites of parity $q$ to those of
parity $p$, and $\psi_e$, $\psi_o$ for the even and odd parts of a field.
The hopping term $K$ connects nearest neighbors, which always have opposite parity, so it has
no even--even or odd--odd block. In $D=C-\tfrac12K(b-cY)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:D}) the diagonal
blocks therefore come from $C$ alone and the off-diagonal ones from the hop alone:
\begin{equation}
D=\begin{pmatrix}D_{ee}&D_{eo}\\D_{oe}&D_{oo}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce & D_{eo}\\ D_{oe} & \Co\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D_{pq}=-\tfrac12K_{pq}(b-cY)\ \ (p\neq q),\qquad
C=\begin{pmatrix}\Ce&0\\0&\Co\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:Dblocks}
\end{equation}
So the $D_{ee}$ and $D_{oo}$ of the even-odd literature (Ref.~\cite{CKMN}, Eq.~(18);
Ref.~\cite{KCM2024}, Eq.~(4)) \emph{are} the site-diagonal part. $D_{ee}=\Ce$ and
$D_{oo}=\Co$ are the blocks of the same $C$ that appears in $A'=(\Dpv\Cpv^{-1})\dg DC^{-1}$,
restricted to the even and to the odd sites. Since $C$ is the same $L_s\times L_s$ matrix
on every site (Sec.~\ref{sec:C}), $\Ce$ and $\Co$ are also the same matrix, applied on
different sites. The same holds for $\Dpv$ with $\Cpve$, $\Cpvo$.

With these blocks the Schur complement on the even sites is $S=\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}$, and
$\Spv$ is that of $\Dpv$. The injection $\iota$ puts a field on the even sites onto the whole
lattice, with zero on the odd sites, and the projection $\pi=\iota\dg$ takes the even sites:
\begin{equation}
\iota y=\begin{pmatrix}y\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\pi\begin{pmatrix}\psi_e\\\psi_o\end{pmatrix}=\psi_e,\qquad
\iota\pi\begin{pmatrix}\psi_e\\\psi_o\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_e\\0\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:iotapi}
\end{equation}
Two identities are used throughout, $S^{-1}=\pi D^{-1}\iota$ and
$(\Spv\dg)^{-1}=\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota$. To see them, solve $D\psi=\iota\eta_e=(\eta_e,0)^{\mathsf T}$. The odd rows give
$\psi_o=-\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\psi_e$, and the even rows then read $S\psi_e=\eta_e$. Hence
$S^{-1}\eta_e=\pi D^{-1}\iota\eta_e$, and the same argument for $\Dpv\dg$ gives the second identity.

\paragraph{Two forms.} The site-diagonal factor can stand on either side of $S$. This gives the
right and the left form,
\begin{equation}
\AR=(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\,(S\,\Ce^{-1}),\qquad
\AL=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,(\Ce^{-1}S).
\label{eq:AR}
\end{equation}
$\AR$ is the even-odd counterpart of $A'$, and $\Ce^{-1}S$ is the usual left-preconditioned even-odd
operator. \New We write $\tilde A$ for either form where a statement holds for both.

\paragraph{With Neff's $\alpha$.} Replace $D$ by $DQ$. Since $Q$ acts within a site and is diagonal
in $s$, the blocks of $DQ$ are $\Ce Q_e$, $D_{eo}Q_o$, $D_{oe}Q_e$ and $\Co Q_o$. Its Schur
complement is therefore
\begin{equation}
\Ce Q_e-D_{eo}Q_o(\Co Q_o)^{-1}D_{oe}Q_e=SQ_e ,
\end{equation}
and its diagonal block is $\Ce Q_e$. In the right form the two factors of $Q_e$ cancel,
$SQ_e(\Ce Q_e)^{-1}=S\Ce^{-1}$. So $\AR$ does not depend on $\alpha$. In the left form they do not
cancel. With $(\Ce Q_e)^{-1}SQ_e=Q_e^{-1}\Ce^{-1}SQ_e$ and $Q$ Hermitian,
\begin{equation}
\AL^{(\alpha)}=
\begin{cases}
Q_e\,(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,Q_e^{-2}\,(\Ce^{-1}S)\,Q_e, & \alpha \text{ in } D \text{ and } \Dpv,\\[2pt]
(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,Q_e^{-1}\,(\Ce^{-1}S)\,Q_e, & \alpha \text{ in } D \text{ only}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ALalpha}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\New Right-hand side and reconstruction.} For $D\psi=\eta$, with
$\eta'_e=\eta_e-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}\eta_o$, the right-hand side is
$\rho_R=(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\eta'_e$ or $\rho_L=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\Ce^{-1}\eta'_e$
(operators with $Q$ when $\alpha\neq1$), and we write $\rho$ for either. The solution $y$ of
$\tilde Ay=\rho$ gives $\psi_e=\Ce^{-1}y$ (right) or $\psi_e=Q_ey$ (left, $y=\psi^{(\alpha)}_e$)
on the even sites and $\psi_o=\Co^{-1}(\eta_o-D_{oe}\psi_e)$ on the odd sites.

\paragraph{Choice of form.} The two forms play complementary roles. \New Because $Q_e$ cancels out of $\AR$, the right-preconditioned form is independent of Neff's
$\alpha$, whereas the left-preconditioned form $\AL^{(\alpha)}$ retains it. As shown in
Sec.~\ref{sec:exactness}, however, the factorized coarse-grid correction of
Sec.~\ref{sec:correction} is exact on the test vectors only for the right form and not for the left
form.\ifresults{} Empirically, with $\alpha=0.6$ the left form matches or improves slightly on the
$\alpha$-independent right form (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}), and reduces iterations modestly relative to
$\AL$ at $\alpha=1$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:massscan}).\fi{} We therefore adopt the left form in
the outer solver.\ifresults{} Unless noted otherwise, all numerical results in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results} employ $\AL^{(\alpha)}$.\fi

\paragraph{$\alpha$ in CG and in the multigrid.} The left-preconditioned Schur factor of $DQ$ is
related to that of $D$ by a similarity transformation,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}=(\Ce Q_e)^{-1}SQ_e=Q_e^{-1}\mathcal KQ_e,\qquad \mathcal K=\Ce^{-1}S .
\label{eq:Ksimilar}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$ and $\mathcal K$ share the same eigenvalues, while their
singular values differ because $Q_e$ is non-unitary. Since the convergence rate of CGNE is governed
by the singular-value spectrum of $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$, varying $\alpha$ directly affects CG at
every quark mass. \New In the multigrid solver, by contrast, $\alpha$ enters
only the outer operator, the smoother, and the site-local factors of the coarse correction; both
the setup and the coarse operators are constructed from the unmodified $D$ and $\Dpv$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), leaving the coarse subspace independent of $\alpha$. Because the low modes
are handled by this coarse subspace rather than through the singular values of
$\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$, one expects $\alpha$ to provide a smaller gain for multigrid than for
CGNE.\ifresults{} This expectation is confirmed numerically in Sec.~\ref{sec:massscan}.\fi

\section{The two-grid method}
\label{sec:method}

A multigrid preconditioner removes the error in two parts. A few steps of an inexpensive fine-grid
\emph{smoother} damp the high modes, while the low modes (which slow down Krylov solvers) are
removed by solving a smaller problem on a coarse lattice. We partition the fine lattice into
four-dimensional blocks of $4^4$ sites, each covering the whole fifth dimension, and map each block
to one coarse site. On each block, the local coarse degrees of freedom are defined by restricting a
set of \emph{test vectors}, \New constructed from approximate low modes of $\AR$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:basis}), to that block. These block-restricted vectors define the prolongation
operator $\Pro$, and the coarse operator of a fine operator \New $\mathcal F$ is its Galerkin projection
$\Pro\dg \mathcal F\Pro$. Because the low modes are locally coherent~\cite{Luscher}, a few vectors per block
suffice; the \emph{setup} cost of computing them is paid once per gauge field. Since the setup uses
only the unmodified operators $D$ and $\Dpv$, while Neff's $\alpha$ enters only the outer operator
$\AL^{(\alpha)}$, its smoother, and \New the site-local factors of the coarse
correction (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction}), one setup applies to both even-odd forms and to every value
of $\alpha$.

\New In Secs.~\ref{sec:correction}--\ref{sec:smoother}, we formulate the
prolongation, coarse operators, coarse solvers, and two-grid cycle for an arbitrary set of
even-site vectors $y_1,\dots,y_n$ approximating the low modes of $\AR$. The setup procedure that
generates and iteratively refines these vectors via Chebyshev filtering and bootstrapping is
detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsection{The factorized coarse-grid correction and the prolongation}
\label{sec:correction}

\subsubsection{Why factorize}
Solving on the even sites halves the problem and improves the conditioning, and red-black CG,
the solver to beat, works there. But the Schur complement reaches two hops, so the existing
methods make a choice (Table~\ref{tab:approaches}). HDCG solves on the even sites and coarsens the
squared Schur complement, with an $81$-point coarse stencil~\cite{HDCG}. The methods with a small
coarse stencil work on all lattice sites. HDCR~\cite{HDCR} coarsens $\gamma_5RD$, Boyle and
Oare~\cite{BoyleOare} coarsen $\Dpv\dg D$, and MG-PV~\cite{BCHW,BY} coarsens $D$ and $\Dpv$
separately on a coarse lattice that keeps the fifth dimension. We keep the separate coarse
operators of $D$ and $\Dpv$, use them for the even-odd operator, and make the coarse lattice
four-dimensional.

\paragraph{The correction.} By $S^{-1}=\pi D^{-1}\iota$, the inverse of the right form is
\begin{equation}
\AR^{-1}=\Ce\,S^{-1}\,(\Spv\dg)^{-1}\,\Cpve\dg
=\Ce\,\pi D^{-1}\iota\;\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota\;\Cpve\dg ,
\label{eq:ARinv}
\end{equation}
a product of two inverses of one-hop operators and site-local factors. \New The
Galerkin operator of $\AR$ itself would have all $81$ points. $S$ reaches two hops, through
$D_{oe}$ and then $D_{eo}$, and $\AR$ contains $S$ and $\Spv\dg$, so it reaches four. The Galerkin
operators of $D$ and $\Dpv$ are nearest-neighbor, since $D$ is nearest-neighbor in four dimensions
and every aggregate contains the whole fifth dimension. \New Through $\pi$ and $\iota$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ARinv} writes the even-site inverses $S^{-1}$ and
$(\Spv\dg)^{-1}$ with the inverses $D^{-1}$ and $(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}$ on the whole lattice, so the
coarse operators need to approximate only the one-hop $D$ and $\Dpv$ (the Schur complement $S$ is two-hop).

\New Before coarsening we write $D^{-1}=B_5^{-1}\Dsym^{-1}$, with $B_5$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:DB5} and
\begin{equation}
\Dsym=DB_5^{-1}=CB_5^{-1}-\tfrac12K
\label{eq:Dsym}
\end{equation}
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:D}. $B_5$ is site-local, so $\Dsym$ reaches one hop, as $D$ does. Unlike $D$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5herm}), $\Dsym$ is $\Gfive$-Hermitian. By $\Gfive B_5\dg\Gfive=B_5$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:b5sim},
\begin{equation}
\Gfive\Dsym\dg\Gfive=\big(\Gfive B_5\dg\Gfive\big)^{-1}\,\Gfive D\dg\Gfive
=B_5^{-1}\,B_5DB_5^{-1}=\Dsym .
\label{eq:Dsymherm}
\end{equation}
Sec.~\ref{sec:coarseops} shows that its coarse operator inherits this property. We therefore
replace each inverse by its Galerkin approximation $\Pro(\Pro\dg \mathcal F\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg$, with
$\mathcal F=\Dsym$ or $\Dpv\dg$. The outer system stays on the even
sites, and the coarse operators are those of the whole lattice; $\iota$ and $\pi$ carry the
residual from the even sites to the whole lattice and back. For both forms and any $\alpha$, the
correction of a residual $r$ on the even sites is
\begin{equation}
e=O_L\;\pi B_5^{-1}\Pro\,\Dc^{-1}\Pro\dg\iota\;\;O_M\;\;
  \pi\Pro\,\big(\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro\big)^{-1}\Pro\dg\iota\;\;O_R\,r,
\qquad \Dc=\Pro\dg\Dsym\Pro .
\label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}

\New The site-local factors $O_R$, $O_M$, $O_L$ of Table~\ref{tab:factors} come from

so $O_R=Q_e^{-1}$, $O_M=\Ce Q_e^2\Cpve\dg$ and $O_L=Q_e^{-1}$. The two coarse inverses are those

\New $B_5$ and $\pi$ commute, since $B_5$ is site-local, so $\pi B_5^{-1}=B_{5,e}^{-1}\pi$. $B_{5,e}^{-1}$ comes

\paragraph{\New Cost of factorized coarsening.} Coarsening the product

\subsubsection{The basis \texorpdfstring{$\{s,\hat t\}$}{s, t}}
\label{sec:basis}
\New The setup (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}) gives vectors $y_1,\dots,y_n$ on the even
sites that approximate low modes of $\AR$. A coarse correction should remove at least these
vectors exactly, so that with exact coarse solves the correction of the residual $r=\AR y$ is $y$.
For a single Galerkin inverse this holds whenever the vector lies in $\operatorname{range}\Pro$.
If \New $\mathcal Fx=b$ and $x=\Pro c$, then
\begin{equation}
\Pro(\Pro\dg \mathcal F\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg b=\Pro(\Pro\dg \mathcal F\Pro)^{-1}\Pro\dg \mathcal F\Pro c=\Pro c=x .
\label{eq:galexact}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr} is a product of two such inverses with $\pi$ and $\iota$ between them.
Sec.~\ref{sec:agg} makes the columns of $\Pro$ from chosen vectors on all lattice sites, and every
chosen vector then lies in $\operatorname{range}\Pro$. So we follow $r=\AR y$ through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr}, find the vector each solve must return, and choose these vectors. The right form has
$O_R=\Cpve\dg$, $O_M=1$, $O_L=\Ce$ (Table~\ref{tab:factors}).

\paragraph{The first solve.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:AR}, the residual after $O_R$ is
\begin{align*}
O_Rr&=\Cpve\dg(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg S\Ce^{-1}y\\
&=\Cpve\dg(\Cpve^{-1})\dg\,\Spv\dg\,S\Ce^{-1}y\\
&=\Spv\dg a,\qquad a\equiv S\Ce^{-1}y .
\end{align*}
The first solve, with $\mathcal F=\Dpv\dg$, gets the right-hand side $\iota\Spv\dg a$. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:galexact} it returns the solution $\hat t$ of
\begin{equation*}
\Dpv\dg\hat t=\iota\Spv\dg a=\begin{pmatrix}\Spv\dg a\\0\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
if $\hat t\in\operatorname{range}\Pro$. We solve for $\hat t$ by eliminating the odd sites, as
in the proof of $(\Spv\dg)^{-1}=\pi(\Dpv\dg)^{-1}\iota$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:eo}). The block form of
$\Dpv\dg$ is the conjugate transpose of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dblocks} for $\Dpv$,
\begin{equation*}
\Dpv\dg=\begin{pmatrix}\Cpve\dg&(D_{\mathrm{PV},oe})\dg\\(D_{\mathrm{PV},eo})\dg&\Cpvo\dg\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The odd rows have zero on the right:
\begin{align*}
(D_{\mathrm{PV},eo})\dg\hat t_e+\Cpvo\dg\hat t_o&=0,\\
\hat t_o&=-(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}(D_{\mathrm{PV},eo})\dg\hat t_e .
\end{align*}
With this $\hat t_o$ the even rows read
\begin{align*}
\Cpve\dg\hat t_e+(D_{\mathrm{PV},oe})\dg\hat t_o
&=\big(\Cpve\dg-(D_{\mathrm{PV},oe})\dg(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}(D_{\mathrm{PV},eo})\dg\big)\hat t_e\\
&=\Spv\dg\hat t_e=\Spv\dg a ,
\end{align*}
so $\hat t_e=a$.

\paragraph{The second solve.} The first solve returned $\hat t$, and $O_M=1$, so the second solve,
with $\mathcal F=D$, gets
\begin{equation*}
\iota O_M\pi\hat t=\iota\hat t_e=\iota a=\begin{pmatrix}S\Ce^{-1}y\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
\New With $\mathcal F=\Dsym$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:galexact} returns the solution $x$ of
$\Dsym x=\iota S\Ce^{-1}y$ if $x\in\operatorname{range}\Pro$. Since $\Dsym x=DB_5^{-1}x$, this is
$x=B_5s$, where $s$ solves $Ds=\iota S\Ce^{-1}y$. We find $s$ by eliminating the odd sites in the same way. With the blocks of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dblocks}, the odd rows give
\begin{align*}
D_{oe}s_e+\Co s_o&=0,\\
s_o&=-\Co^{-1}D_{oe}s_e ,
\end{align*}
and the even rows then read
\begin{align*}
\Ce s_e+D_{eo}s_o&=\big(\Ce-D_{eo}\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\big)s_e\\
&=Ss_e=S\Ce^{-1}y ,
\end{align*}
so $s_e=\Ce^{-1}y$. The last factors give the correction,
\begin{equation*}
e=O_L\pi B_5^{-1}B_5s=\Ce s_e=\Ce\Ce^{-1}y=y .
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{The two lifts.} The two solves thus need the vectors
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s&=\big(\Ce^{-1}y,\;-\Co^{-1}D_{oe}\Ce^{-1}y\big),\\
\hat t&=\big(a,\;-(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}(D_{\mathrm{PV},eo})\dg a\big),\qquad a=S\Ce^{-1}y=\pi Ds ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lifts}
\end{equation}
where $a=\pi Ds$ because $Ds=\iota S\Ce^{-1}y$. The setup uses this form, building $s$ from $y$,
applying $D$ once and taking the even sites to get $a$ without applying the two-hop $S$. We call $s$ and $\hat t$ the two \emph{lifts} of $y$.
\New The second solve returns $B_5s$, which the setup gets from $s$ by the site-local $B_5$.
Each extends a field on the even sites to all lattice sites by solving the odd rows of one
operator, as $\psi_o$ is rebuilt from $\psi_e$ after an even-odd solve. The correction returns $y$
when \New $B_5s$ and $\hat t$ lie in $\operatorname{range}\Pro$. Sec.~\ref{sec:agg} builds $\Pro$ from them, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:exactness} states the result.

\subsubsection{Aggregation and prolongation}
\label{sec:agg}
\New The coarse space is defined in three steps. We partition the lattice into blocks, assign a
coarse vector with a few components to each block, and define the map $\Pro$ from such a vector to
a fine field.
We divide the four-dimensional lattice into rectangular blocks $X$; the blocks are fixed before
$\Pro$ and do not depend on the operators. A block is not cut in the fifth dimension, so it
holds all $(x,s)$ with $x\in X$ and $s=0,\dots,L_s-1$. With $4^4$ blocks on $24^3\times48$ there
are $6^3\times12$ blocks, and a fine field has $4^4\times L_s\times12$ complex components on each.
The \emph{coarse lattice} has one site for each block, and two coarse sites are neighbors in
direction $\mu$ when their blocks are. A coarse vector $c$ has $2n_v$ complex components
$c_{X,1},\dots,c_{X,2n_v}$ on each coarse site $X$. On each block we construct below $2n_v$
orthonormal fine fields $q_{X,1},\dots,q_{X,2n_v}$, zero outside $X$, and define
\begin{equation}
(\Pro c)(x,s)=\sum_{l=1}^{2n_v}c_{X,l}\,q_{X,l}(x,s)\quad\text{for }x\in X,\qquad
(\Pro\dg\psi)_{X,l}=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{s}q_{X,l}(x,s)\dg\,\psi(x,s).
\label{eq:Pdef}
\end{equation}
So $\Pro$ represents a fine field on a block by its $2n_v$ coefficients along the $q_{X,l}$, and
$\Pro\dg\psi$ collects the projections of $\psi$ on them.

The coarse vector with the single component $c_{X,l}=1$ is mapped to $q_{X,l}$, so the
$q_{X,l}$ are the columns of $\Pro$.

The $q_{X,l}$ are made from the two lifts of each of the $n$ vectors $y_j$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:basis}. We call these
$2n$ vectors \New $B_5s_1,\dots,B_5s_n,\hat t_1,\dots,\hat t_n$ the \emph{test vectors}; each is a vector
on all lattice sites, and their number is $n_v=2n$. Below, $v$ stands for any one of them. We split each test vector $v$ into its $\Gfive$ eigencomponents
$v_\pm=\frac12(1\pm\Gfive)v$ and cut each of them into pieces, one per block, each equal to $v_\pm$
on the sites of its block and zero elsewhere. For one block and one chirality, the pieces of the
$n_v$ test vectors are the columns \New $z_k$ of a matrix $Z$. We orthonormalize them, $Z=\hat Q\hat R$ with $\hat R$
upper triangular. The $n_v$ columns of $\hat Q$, zero outside the block, are
$q_{X,1},\dots,q_{X,n_v}$ for the positive chirality and $q_{X,n_v+1},\dots,q_{X,2n_v}$ for the
negative one. So the first $n_v$ components of a coarse site belong to positive and the last
$n_v$ to negative $\Gfive$-chirality.

\New By construction, every test vector $v=v_++v_-$ lies in
$\operatorname{range}\Pro$, since its restriction to each block and chirality is a column
$z_k=\sum_l q_{X,l}\hat R_{lk}$ of $Z=\hat Q\hat R$, and thus a linear combination of the columns of $\Pro$.

As in the chirality split of Wilson multigrid~\cite{Babich}, each column of $\Pro$ is then an
eigenvector of $\Gfive$.\footnote{\New Two choices keep this property. $\Gfive$ acts within one
four-dimensional site, and each block holds the whole fifth dimension, so the restriction of
$v_\pm$ to a block is still an eigenvector of $\Gfive$. The $+$ and $-$ parts are orthonormalized
separately, and a linear combination of eigenvectors with one eigenvalue is again an eigenvector
with that eigenvalue.} \New Since $\Gfive=\gamma_5R$, the condition $\Gfive v_\pm=\pm v_\pm$ reads
$v_\pm(L_s-1-s)=\pm\gamma_5v_\pm(s)$. So each part is fixed by its values for $s<L_s/2$, and
$\Pro$ is stored only there, at half the memory. On the even sites the transfer operators are $\Pro\dg\iota$ and
$\pi\Pro$.

\paragraph{\New CholeskyQR2.} We compute $Z=\hat Q\hat R$ through the Gram matrix $\hat G=Z\dg Z=\hat R\dg\hat R$, so
the Cholesky factor of $\hat G$ is $\hat R$, and $\hat Q=Z\hat R^{-1}$ needs one Gram matrix, one Cholesky factor and
one triangular solve. \New One pass loses orthogonality like the square of the condition number of
$Z$~\cite{CholQR2}, so we do it twice; the second Gram matrix is close to the identity. Each pass reads $Z$
twice, where Gram--Schmidt reads it once per pair of columns. Where a pivot $\hat R_{jj}^2$ of the
first pass falls below $10^{-12}\,\hat G_{jj}$, the block's vectors are nearly dependent and we use
Gram--Schmidt instead.

\subsubsection{\New Exactness on the test vectors}
\label{sec:exactness}
\paragraph{The right form.} Let $\Dc$
and $\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro$ be invertible, and let both coarse systems in Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr} be
solved exactly. Then the right-form
correction returns the vector it is built from,
\begin{equation}
e(\AR y)=y .
\label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
\New The two solves of Sec.~\ref{sec:basis} return $\hat t$ and $B_5s$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:galexact}, because both lie in $\operatorname{range}\Pro$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:agg}), and
the correction then returns $y$.

\paragraph{\New Why both lifts.} With $\Pro$ made from the $B_5s_j$ alone, the
first solve returns $\hat t$ only if $\hat t$ lies in the span of the $s_j$. Otherwise its result
differs from $\hat t$, the second solve receives a right-hand side other than $Ds$, and $e\neq y$
in general. The two lifts differ in the fifth dimension. Their odd parts are built
with $\Co^{-1}$ and with $(\Cpvo\dg)^{-1}$, and by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Cinv} and the transposed blocks
of $C\dg$ these carry each chirality toward opposite ends of the fifth dimension. We therefore
expect $\hat t$ not to lie in that span.\ifresults{} Sec.~\ref{sec:basischoice} measures the
effect of leaving $\hat t$ out.\fi

\paragraph{In practice.} Equation~\eqref{eq:exact} holds only approximately, because the stored $\Pro$
contains $s$ and $\hat t$ only up to the rounding of its reduced-precision storage, the coarse
solves stop at a loose tolerance, and the Pauli--Villars solve is regularized.

\paragraph{The left form.} \New We follow the left form in the same way, first at $\alpha=1$.
There $O_R=1$, $O_M=\Ce\Cpve\dg$, $O_L=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:factors}), and we take the residual
$r=\AL y'$ with $y'=\Ce^{-1}y$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:AR}, with $a=S\Ce^{-1}y=Sy'$ as before,
\begin{equation*}
O_Rr=(\Cpve^{-1}\Spv)\dg\,\Ce^{-1}Sy'=\Spv\dg u,\qquad u\equiv(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}\Ce^{-1}a .
\end{equation*}
The first coarse solve must therefore return the Pauli--Villars lift $\hat t'$ of $u$, constructed
from $u$ in the same manner as $\hat t$ is constructed from $a$. Its even-site component is $u$, so
the second solve receives $\iota O_Mu=\iota\Ce\Cpve\dg(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}\Ce^{-1}a=\iota a$, identical
to the right-form case; it therefore returns $B_5s$, giving \New $e=O_L\pi B_5^{-1}B_5s=\Ce^{-1}y=y'$. Exactness for
the left form thus requires $\hat t'\in\operatorname{range}\Pro$. However, because $u$ differs from
$a$ by the site-local matrix $(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}\Ce^{-1}$, which couples spin and the fifth dimension,
$\hat t'$ does not generally lie in the span of \New $\{B_5s_j,\hat t_j\}$, and the correction is not exact
for the left form. With $\alpha$ introduced in both operators, the same algebra yields
$u=(\Cpve\dg)^{-1}Q_e^{-2}\Ce^{-1}SQ_ey'$ and additionally requires the quark lift associated with
$Q_ey'$.\ifresults{} In practice, the left form nevertheless converges cleanly, exhibiting nearly
the same spectrum as the right form at $\alpha=1$ (\New Appendix~\ref{sec:spectrum}) and still benefiting
substantially from including $\hat t$ in the basis (Sec.~\ref{sec:basischoice}).\fi

\subsubsection{The coarse operators}
\label{sec:coarseops}
We assemble $\Dc=\Pro\dg\Dsym\Pro$ and $\Dcpv=\Pro\dg\Dpv\Pro$ from the hops of the fine operator
between neighboring blocks and from its part within a block. \New By Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dsym}, the
hop of $\Dsym$ is $-\frac12K$, that of Shamir's operator, and its part within a site is the
$L_s\times L_s$ matrix $CB_5^{-1}$ on each chirality. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Pdef} the entries of $\Dc$ are $q_{X',k}\dg\Dsym q_{X,l}$. $\Dsym$ moves a field by at most
one site in four dimensions, so $\Dsym q_{X,l}$ vanishes outside $X$ and its neighbors, and the entry
vanishes unless $X'=X$ or $X'$ is a neighbor of $X$. Each coarse site has a
$2n_v\times2n_v$ diagonal block and one link block for each of its eight nearest neighbors, nine blocks in all. The
adjoint factor $\Pro\dg\Dpv\dg\Pro=(\Dcpv)\dg$ is the conjugate transpose of these blocks.
\New Every column of $\Pro$ is an eigenvector of $\Gfive$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:agg}), so
$\Gfive\Pro=\Pro\sigma_3$, where $\sigma_3$ is $+1$ on the first $n_v$ and $-1$ on the last $n_v$
components of each coarse site. With Eq.~\eqref{eq:Dsymherm},
\begin{equation}
\Dc\dg=\Pro\dg\Dsym\dg\Pro=\Pro\dg\Gfive\Dsym\Gfive\Pro=\sigma_3\Dc\sigma_3 ,
\end{equation}
so $\Dc$ is $\sigma_3$-Hermitian. The same steps do not apply to $\Dcpv$, since $\Dpv$ is not
$\Gfive$-Hermitian (Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5herm} at $m=1$). When all
extents of the coarse lattice are even, the coarse hops connect coarse sites of opposite parity,
and both coarse operators have an even-odd form of their own.

\subsection{The coarse solvers}
\label{sec:coarse}

We solve both coarse systems only approximately, in reduced precision, to a loose relative
residual or a maximum number of iterations. Reaching the tolerance is not required. The two are
solved in sequence, starting with the Pauli--Villars factor, whose solution enters the right-hand side of
the quark factor. The default, used for all results unless stated, is BiCGStab on the even-odd
form of the quark factor and CG on the regularized normal equation~\eqref{eq:tikhonov} of the
Pauli--Villars factor, both to $|r|^2/|b|^2<10^{-2}$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform}).

\subsubsection{The quark factor}
Write $\Dc=\begin{pmatrix}A_{ee}&B_{eo}\\ B_{oe}&A_{oo}\end{pmatrix}$ on the
coarse lattice. The system $\Dc c=g$ is solved through its Schur complement,
\begin{equation}
\big(A_{ee}-B_{eo}A_{oo}^{-1}B_{oe}\big)\,c_e=g_e-B_{eo}A_{oo}^{-1}g_o,\qquad
c_o=A_{oo}^{-1}(g_o-B_{oe}c_e),
\label{eq:coarseeo}
\end{equation}
with right-preconditioned BiCGStab~\cite{vanderVorst,SleijpenvdVomega} or truncated flexible GCR,
preconditioned by the exact inverses of the diagonal blocks $A_{ee}^{-1}$
(Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform}).

\subsubsection{The Pauli--Villars factor}
\New Unlike the light-quark operator $D$, the fine Pauli--Villars operator $\Dpv=D|_{m=1}$ has
unit mass and no critical slowing down. Its Galerkin coarse factor $\mathcal F=(\Dcpv)\dg$,
however, can acquire singular values far smaller than those of $\Dpv$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:pvcoarse}),
so an unregularized solve of $\mathcal Fw=g$ strongly amplifies those directions and can cause the
outer solve to slow down or stagnate.

\New The Galerkin projection bounds the small singular values of a coarse operator only through
the \emph{field of values} $W(M)=\{v\dg Mv/v\dg v : v\neq0\}$ of the fine operator $M$. Since the
$q_{X,l}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Pdef} are orthonormal and the blocks are disjoint, $\Pro\dg\Pro=1$ and
$|\Pro c|=|c|$. For a coarse vector $c$ with $|c|=1$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
\begin{equation*}
|\Pro\dg M\Pro\,c|\;\ge\;|c\dg\Pro\dg M\Pro\,c|\;=\;|(\Pro c)\dg M(\Pro c)|\;\ge\;
\min_{z\in W(M)}|z|,
\end{equation*}
since $(\Pro c)\dg M(\Pro c)$ is a point of $W(M)$. Minimizing over $c$ gives
$\sigma_{\min}(\Pro\dg M\Pro)\ge\min_{z\in W(M)}|z|$. \New For a Hermitian positive definite $M$,
$W(M)$ is the interval between its smallest and largest eigenvalues, so the bound equals
$\sigma_{\min}(M)$ and the coarse operator has no singular value below the smallest one of $M$.
\New $\Dpv$ is not Hermitian, and the bound is only the distance of $W(\Dpv)$ from the origin. A
coarse singular value near zero means a unit vector $x=\Pro c$ with $\Pro\dg\Dpv x\approx0$ although
$|\Dpv x|\ge\sigma_{\min}(\Dpv)$, so $\Dpv x$ is nearly orthogonal to the range of $\Pro$. Then
$x\dg\Dpv x=c\dg\Pro\dg\Dpv x\approx0$ is a point of $W(\Dpv)$ near the origin, which the bound
permits.\ifresults{} Table~\ref{tab:pvsv} shows that this occurs with smeared links, where the coarse
smallest singular value falls far below that of $\Dpv$.\fi

\New We solve the normal equation, which is Hermitian positive definite and so solvable by CG,
with a Tikhonov shift $\tau$ that damps the directions with small singular values:
\begin{equation}
\big(\mathcal F\dg \mathcal F+\tau\big)\,w=\mathcal F\dg g
\label{eq:tikhonov}
\end{equation}
on all coarse sites by unpreconditioned CG, with $\tau=10^{-3}$.

\New With the singular value decomposition $\mathcal F=\sum_i\sigma_iu_iv_i\dg$, the exact inverse
weights each right singular vector $v_i$ by $(u_i\dg g)/\sigma_i$, whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:tikhonov}
gives
\begin{equation*}
w=\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\tau}(u_i\dg g)\,v_i
=\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2+\tau}\,\frac{u_i\dg g}{\sigma_i}\,v_i .
\end{equation*}
Compared with the exact solve, the shift multiplies each component by $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\tau)$,
leaving components with $\sigma_i\gg\sqrt\tau$ nearly unchanged and damping those with
$\sigma_i\ll\sqrt\tau$. By $\sigma_i^2+\tau\ge2\sigma_i\sqrt\tau$, it caps the amplification factor
$\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\tau)$ at $1/(2\sqrt\tau)$ (about $16$ at $\tau=10^{-3}$). We use neither
even-odd reduction nor further coarse levels for this factor.

\subsubsection{Levels below the coarse lattice}
\New On large volumes, we precondition the quark coarse system by further aggregation levels below
the coarse lattice; inverting the last level exactly by double-precision LU treats every mode that
reaches it without computing eigenvectors for explicit deflation. Labeling the coarse lattice as
level $1$ with operator $\mathcal D_1=\Dc$, we group the sites of level $\ell$ into rectangular
super-blocks, as the fine sites are grouped into blocks in Sec.~\ref{sec:agg}, so that each
super-block becomes one site of level $\ell+1$. On $24^3\times48$, super-blocks of $2^4$ and then
$3^4$ sites reduce the $6^3\times12$ coarse lattice to $3^3\times6$ and $1^3\times2$.

\New On each super-block, local basis vectors are formed from the level's test vectors, starting
with $\Pro\dg s_j$ and $\Pro\dg\hat t_j$ at level $1$, split by chirality and orthonormalized by
Gram--Schmidt applied twice. These define the prolongation $\Pro_{\ell+1}$ from level $\ell+1$ to
level $\ell$ and the Galerkin operator
$\mathcal D_{\ell+1}=\Pro_{\ell+1}\dg\mathcal D_\ell\Pro_{\ell+1}$, which remains nearest-neighbor.
Its blocks are extracted by probing. We apply $\mathcal D_{\ell+1}$ to sources nonzero only on sites
spaced far enough apart that their images do not overlap, and read each block off the image.

\New With $B_\ell$ the diagonal blocks of level $\ell$ and exact dense LU on the last level, the
multilevel preconditioner is a V-cycle with the coarse correction followed by one block-Jacobi sweep
on the updated residual, without pre-smoothing:
\begin{equation}
z=\Pro_{\ell+1}M_{\ell+1}\Pro_{\ell+1}\dg r,\qquad
M_\ell r=z+B_\ell^{-1}(r-\mathcal D_\ell z).
\end{equation}
With the even-odd coarse form, BiCGStab solves the Schur system of Eq.~\eqref{eq:coarseeo} on the
even coarse sites, and its preconditioner extends the even-site vector by zero to the odd coarse
sites, applies $M_1$, and keeps the even part. By the identity of Sec.~\ref{sec:eo} on the coarse
lattice, the even-even block of $\Dc^{-1}$ is the inverse of the Schur complement, so the even part
of an approximation of $\Dc^{-1}$ approximates that inverse.

\subsection{The Chebyshev smoother and the cycle}
\label{sec:smoother}

\subsubsection{Smoother}
\New The smoother reduces the components of the residual in the upper part of the spectrum of
$\tilde A$ and leaves those near $0$ to the coarse correction. It is $\nu$ steps of Chebyshev
iteration~\cite{Manteuffel,Saad} for $\tilde Ax=b$ from $x=0$, a fixed polynomial $x=p(\tilde A)b$
of degree $\nu-1$. The polynomial is defined by an interval $[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ on
the real axis, where $\lambda_{\rm hi}$ lies slightly above an estimate of the spectral radius of
$\tilde A$ from a power iteration and $\lambda_{\rm lo}=\lambda_{\rm hi}/\kappa_s$ for a chosen
ratio $\kappa_s$. With \New $\bar\lambda=\frac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}+\lambda_{\rm lo})$,
$\delta=\frac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}-\lambda_{\rm lo})$ and $\sigma=\bar\lambda/\delta$, the residual after
the $\nu$ steps is $b-\tilde Ax=q_\nu(\tilde A)\,b$ with
\begin{equation*}
q_\nu(\lambda)=\frac{T_\nu\big((\bar\lambda-\lambda)/\delta\big)}{T_\nu(\sigma)}
\end{equation*}
\cite{Saad}, \New where $T_\nu$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree $\nu$. For $\lambda$ in
$[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ the argument $(\bar\lambda-\lambda)/\delta$ lies in $[-1,1]$, so
$|q_\nu(\lambda)|\le1/T_\nu(\sigma)$, while $q_\nu(0)=1$. For real eigenvalues the smoother thus
multiplies the components in the interval by at most $1/T_\nu(\sigma)$, about $1/94$ for $\nu=8$
and $\kappa_s=10$ and $1/42$ for $\nu=12$ and $\kappa_s=30$, and leaves those near $0$ almost
unchanged.

$\tilde A$ is not Hermitian, so its eigenvalues are in general complex. Chebyshev iteration can
then be defined by an ellipse in the complex plane with foci $\lambda_{\rm lo}$ and
$\lambda_{\rm hi}$~\cite{Manteuffel}; we use the degenerate ellipse, the interval itself, and
choose $\kappa_s$ by hand.\ifresults{} An interval that is too wide or too narrow can make the outer
solve stagnate, and the interval that works differs between the two setups of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results} (Secs.~\ref{sec:setups} and \ref{sec:sensitivity}).\fi

\New Evaluating $x=p(\tilde A)b$ by the Chebyshev recurrence of Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cheb_rec}, costs $\nu-1$ applications of $\tilde A$, since the last step only updates
$x$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:cheb_rec} keeps four fine vectors regardless of $\nu$ and needs no inner
products, so the smoother is the same linear map at every application. A Krylov smoother, such as
the fixed number of GCR steps of the Pauli--Villars multigrid of Ref.~\cite{BY}, keeps two more
fine vectors per step, the direction and its image under $\tilde A$; in a fixed amount of memory it
therefore solves smaller volumes.

\subsubsection{Cycle}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Two-grid cycle $y=\mathcal Mb$ for $\tilde A$.}
\label{alg:cycle}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State $y\gets p(\tilde A)\,b$ \Comment{pre-smoothing}
\State $r\gets b-\tilde Ay$
\State $y\gets y+e(r)$ \Comment{correction~\eqref{eq:corr}}
\State $r\gets b-\tilde Ay$
\State $y\gets y+p(\tilde A)\,r$ \Comment{post-smoothing}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

One cycle costs $2(\nu-1)+2=2\nu$ applications of $\tilde A$ (two smoothings of $\nu-1$ each
and two residuals) plus the two coarse solves. \New One application of $\tilde A$ is two
Schur factors, $S$ and $\Spv\dg$, of two hops each, four hops in all, so $\nu=8$ means
$16\times4=64$ applications of $D_{eo}$ or $D_{oe}$ per cycle. One CG iteration on $\mathcal K\dg\mathcal K$ has four, so the hops of one cycle are those of
$16$ CG iterations, before the coarse solves. Because the coarse solves are Krylov
iterations stopped by a residual test, \New whose coefficients and number of steps depend on the
right-hand side, $\mathcal M$ is not a fixed linear map.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The setup produces the vectors $y_1,\dots,y_n$ on the even sites, then the basis
$\{s_j,\hat t_j\}$, $\Pro$ and the coarse operators. It always uses the right form; the same
setup serves the left forms and every $\alpha$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction}). It has two stages.
A Chebyshev filter finds approximate low modes without any multigrid. A bootstrap then improves
them with the cycle $\mathcal M$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cycle} built from them, so that they also
cover what the first coarse space misses.
\New The filter comes first because the cycle is built from such vectors and none exist at the
start. Because the filter degree grows like the inverse square root of the lowest eigenvalue of
$\AR\dg\AR$ to be separated (Sec.~\ref{sec:filter}), reaching the lowest modes with the filter
alone requires a high polynomial degree. \New A bootstrap round instead costs a fixed number of
FGMRES steps per vector, whatever that eigenvalue (Sec.~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), so once an initial
cycle exists, a few rounds refine the vectors further.

\subsubsection{Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration}
\label{sec:filter}
The vectors $y_1,\dots,y_n$ approximate the right singular vectors of the smallest singular values of $\AR$,
that is the eigenvectors of the lowest eigenvalues of the Hermitian positive operator \New $H=\AR\dg\AR$.
$H$ acts on the even sites of the whole lattice; the blocks enter only
afterwards, when $\Pro$ is made from the $y_j$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:agg}). No cycle exists yet at this
stage, so we enlarge the low-mode components of $n$ vectors by a polynomial in $H$, which
needs only applications of $\AR$ and $\AR\dg$.

\New Let $H=\sum_i\mu_iv_iv_i\dg$ be the eigendecomposition of $H$, with
$\mu_i=\sigma_i^2$ and $v_i$ the right singular vectors of $\AR$. Applying a polynomial filter
$p_f(H)$ to $n$ random initial vectors rescales each eigencomponent $v_i$ by $p_f(\mu_i)$. To
suppress the high-mode interval $[\mu_{\rm lo},\mu_{\rm hi}]$ of $H$, where $\mu_{\rm hi}$ is
chosen slightly above the largest eigenvalue $\mu_{\max}(H)$ via power iteration and $\mu_{\rm lo}$
starts at a small fraction of $\mu_{\rm hi}$, we map $[\mu_{\rm lo},\mu_{\rm hi}]$ onto $[-1,1]$ by
\begin{equation*}
\ell(\mu)=\frac{2\mu-(\mu_{\rm hi}+\mu_{\rm lo})}{\mu_{\rm hi}-\mu_{\rm lo}},
\end{equation*}
and set $p_f=T_d\circ\ell$, where $T_d$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree $d$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:smoother}). Then
$|p_f(\mu)|\le1$ on $[\mu_{\rm lo},\mu_{\rm hi}]$, whereas for $\mu<\mu_{\rm lo}$
we have $\ell(\mu)<-1$ and $|p_f(\mu)|=\cosh(d\,\mathrm{arccosh}|\ell(\mu)|)$ grows
exponentially with $d$. At $\mu=0$,
$|\ell(0)|=1+2\mu_{\rm lo}/(\mu_{\rm hi}-\mu_{\rm lo})$, which gives an amplification
factor $\approx\cosh\big(2d\sqrt{\mu_{\rm lo}/\mu_{\rm hi}}\big)$ for
$\mu_{\rm lo}\ll\mu_{\rm hi}$; maintaining a fixed amplification therefore requires
$d\propto\sqrt{\mu_{\rm hi}/\mu_{\rm lo}}$. We evaluate $p_f(H)$ through the standard
three-term recurrence $T_{k+1}(t)=2tT_k(t)-T_{k-1}(t)$ ($T_0=1$, $T_1=t$), at a cost of one
application of $H$ (one each of $\AR$ and $\AR\dg$) per degree.

\New Algorithm~\ref{alg:lowmodes} iterates this filtering step over multiple
rounds. After each filtering pass, the $n$ vectors are orthonormalized and projected via a
Rayleigh--Ritz diagonalization of $H$; the subsequent round sets $\mu_{\rm lo}$ to the
largest resulting Ritz value so as to concentrate amplification below the current subspace.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{\textsc{LowModes}: Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration on \New $H=\AR\dg\AR$.}
\label{alg:lowmodes}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State \New $\Phi\gets$ $n$ random vectors on the even sites
\State $\mu_{\rm hi}\gets$ a bound slightly above $\mu_{\max}(H)$ (power iteration);\quad $\mu_{\rm lo}\gets$ a small fraction of $\mu_{\rm hi}$
\For{each round}
  \State $d\gets$ a degree proportional to $\sqrt{\mu_{\rm hi}/\mu_{\rm lo}}$, within fixed bounds
  \State $\Phi\gets T_d(\ell(H))\,\Phi$;\quad orthonormalize $\Phi$ through its Gram matrix
  \State $\Phi\dg H\Phi=\Xi\Theta\Xi\dg$;\quad $\Phi\gets\Phi\Xi$ \Comment{Rayleigh--Ritz}
  \State $\mu_{\rm lo}\gets\theta_{\max}$ \Comment{largest Ritz value}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\Phi$, $\Theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Bootstrap}
\label{sec:bootstrap}
\New A bootstrap round is one step of inverse iteration that replaces each
$y_j$ by an approximation of $\AR^{-1}y_j$, which the two-grid cycle $\mathcal M$ of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cycle} built from the current vectors makes affordable. With the singular value
decomposition $\AR=\sum_i\sigma_iu_iv_i\dg$,
\begin{equation*}
\AR^{-1}y_j=\sum_iv_i\,\frac{u_i\dg y_j}{\sigma_i}.
\end{equation*}
Provided $u_i\dg y_j$ is not small, we therefore expect the new vectors to weight most heavily the
right singular vectors $v_i$ of small $\sigma_i$, which are the low modes the setup seeks. A round
has three steps. It builds the two-lift basis $\{s_j,\hat t_j\}$, the prolongation $\Pro$, the
coarse operators (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction}), and the two-grid cycle $\mathcal M$ from the current
$y$. It replaces every $y_j$ by $n_{\rm fg}=4$ FGMRES steps~\cite{SaadFGMRES}
(Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform}) on $\AR x=y_j$ from $x=0$, preconditioned by $\mathcal M$. And it
orthonormalizes the new $y$ through their Gram matrix ($y\leftarrow yV\Lambda^{-1/2}$ with
$y\dg y=V\Lambda V\dg$).

\paragraph{\New Number of rounds.}
\label{sec:autoboot}
\New The bootstrap stops when a probe of the next round, described below, predicts that the round
would lower the lowest Ritz value of $H=\AR\dg\AR$ on $\operatorname{span}(y)$ by less than a factor
$g_{\min}$ (we take $g_{\min}=4$).
After round $r$ the $n$ vectors $y$ are orthonormal, so the Ritz values are the eigenvalues of the
$n\times n$ matrix $(\AR y)\dg(\AR y)$. We call the lowest one $\theta_r$ and its Ritz vector
$\phi_r$; the filter gives $\theta_0$ and $\phi_0$, and computing them costs one application of
$\AR$ per vector. We monitor $\theta_r$ rather than the residual $|H\phi_r-\theta_r\phi_r|$,
because at small quark masses $\theta_r$ falls below single precision relative to $\mu_{\max}(H)$,
so the residual stagnates while $\theta_r$ still decreases.

\New Updating all $n$ vectors in a full round is costly, so before committing to round $r+1$ we
probe its cycle $\mathcal M_{r+1}$ on the single vector $\phi_r$. We use the next-round cycle
$\mathcal M_{r+1}$ built from the current $y$, because $\mathcal M_r$ is built from the earlier
vectors and cannot gauge the improvement gained in round $r$. Applying the same $n_{\rm fg}=4$
FGMRES steps to $\phi_r$ alone gives
\begin{equation}
x=\textsc{Fgmres}_{n_{\rm fg}}(\AR,\mathcal M_{r+1},\phi_r),\qquad
\tilde\theta_{r+1}=\frac{|\AR x|^2}{|x|^2},\qquad g_{r+1}=\frac{\theta_r}{\tilde\theta_{r+1}}.
\label{eq:probe}
\end{equation}
Here $\tilde\theta_{r+1}$ is the Rayleigh quotient of $H$ at the updated vector $x$. The ratio
$g_{r+1}$ is the gain of the probe; it equals the reduction factor of $\theta_r$ if the full round
improves the subspace as much as the probe improves $\phi_r$. If $g_{r+1}<g_{\min}$, the round is
skipped and the bootstrap stops with $y$ unchanged; because $\mathcal M_{r+1}$ is already built
from the final vectors, it is kept directly for the outer solve. Otherwise, every vector $y_j$ is
updated using $\mathcal M_{r+1}$.

\New We allow at most $8$ rounds. The first $r_{\min}=2$ rounds run without probing, because on
small coarse spaces or with few smoother steps the second-round cycle can lower the Rayleigh
quotient of $\phi_1$ by less than $g_{\min}$ even when the vectors are still far from the lowest
modes\ifresults{} (Sec.~\ref{sec:autorounds})\fi. Compared with a fixed number of rounds, the probe
adds only $n_{\rm fg}$ FGMRES steps on a single vector and one Ritz-pair evaluation per round;
Sec.~\ref{sec:autorounds} gives the round counts chosen in practice. Algorithm~\ref{alg:setup} puts
the filter, the rounds and the probe together.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Setup of the two-grid method. \New With a fixed number of rounds $R$, the loop runs $R$ times without the test.}
\label{alg:setup}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State $y\gets\textsc{LowModes}(H)$;\quad $(\theta,\phi)\gets$ lowest Ritz pair of $H$ on $y$
\For{$r=0,\dots,7$}
  \State \New from $y$, build $\Pro$ (\New $B_5s_j$ and $\hat t_j$ from the lifts of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lifts}, split by $\Gfive$ and orthonormalized per block), $\Dc$ and $\Dcpv$ with the inverses of their diagonal blocks and their even-odd forms, optionally the levels below, and the cycle $\mathcal M$
  \If{$r\ge r_{\min}$ and $\theta<g_{\min}\,\tilde\theta$ for $\tilde\theta$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:probe}} \textbf{break} \Comment{\New keep $\Pro$, $\mathcal M$}
  \EndIf
  \For{$j=1,\dots,n$}
    \State $y_j\gets n_{\rm fg}$ FGMRES steps on $\AR x=y_j$, preconditioned by $\mathcal M$
  \EndFor
  \State orthonormalize $y$;\quad $(\theta,\phi)\gets$ lowest Ritz pair of $H$ on $y$
\EndFor
\If{the loop did not end by the test} \Comment{\New the last round changed $y$}
  \State build $\Pro$, the coarse operators and $\mathcal M$ from the final $y$, as at the top of the loop
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The outer solver}
\label{sec:outer}

\New The outer solver solves the even-odd system $\tilde Ay=\rho$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:eo}, with
$\tilde A=\AR$ or $\AL$ and $\rho$ its right-hand side. It applies the cycle $\mathcal M$ of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:cycle} to each residual $r$ and builds $y$ from the directions $\mathcal Mr$;
$\mathcal M$ thus acts as a right preconditioner of $\tilde A$, a term unrelated to the right and
left forms. From $y$ we obtain $\psi_e$ and $\psi_o$ as at the end of Sec.~\ref{sec:eo}. The cycle $\mathcal M$ changes from one application to the
next, so the outer method must be flexible, such as FGMRES~\cite{SaadFGMRES} or GCR~\cite{ESS} in its
flexible form (Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform}), which keep the preconditioned directions.

\New For solves with $D$ we keep $k=2$ directions, GCR(2), on every volume, so that the volumes
compare directly. With GCR(2) the whole multigrid on $24^3\times48$ needs $20$--$23$\,GB, so it runs
on one GPU with $24$\,GB of device memory. Four choices keep it small: the coarse operators have
nine points (Table~\ref{tab:approaches}), $\Pro$ is stored in half precision and only for
$s<L_s/2$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:agg}), the Chebyshev smoother keeps four fine vectors
(Sec.~\ref{sec:smoother}), and GCR(2) keeps two directions. Each further direction costs two fine
vectors, about $1$\,GB on $24^3\times48$.\ifresults{} A larger $k$ changes only the outer solver and
leaves the cycle as it is; on $12^4$ it changes the median iterations by at most one
(Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}).\fi

Flexibility alone gives no convergence guarantee. For a fixed $\mathcal M$, restarted or truncated
methods converge for every restart length if the field of values of $\tilde A\mathcal M$ lies in
the right half-plane~\cite{ESS}. Otherwise only the full method is covered, by the spectrum and
the pseudospectra of $\tilde A\mathcal M$~\cite{NRT,TrefethenEmbree}, and a restarted method can
stagnate~\cite{Embree}; for truncated GCR, Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform} gives the fixed point at which it stalls.

The reference solver is CG on the normal equation of the even-odd operator $\Ce^{-1}S$ (CGNE). Its
operator $(\Ce^{-1}S)\dg\Ce^{-1}S$ is Hermitian positive definite for any nonsingular $S$, so CG
converges, but its condition number is the square of that of $\Ce^{-1}S$. It runs in double
precision, or in mixed precision using CG in single precision with reliable updates in double
precision.\ifresults{} Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform} compares it with CG on $S\dg S$.\fi

\New Unless stated otherwise, every solve stops on the residual of the original equation,
$|D\psi-\eta|^2/|\eta|^2$, with the plain $D$ after reconstruction. The multigrid and CGNE iterate
on different even-odd systems, and this test makes them stop on the same quantity.

\section{Solving with \texorpdfstring{$D\dg$}{D-dagger}}
\label{sec:dagger}

HMC trajectories require $(D\dg D)^{-1}$ at every force evaluation, so solves with $D\dg$ occur as
often as those with $D$. The multigrid hierarchy built for $D$ can also be used for $D\dg$ without
a second setup, coarse operator, or cycle. \New We consider two routes. When the
M\"obius coefficients $b$ and $c$ are independent of $s$, as in this work, an exact similarity
relation maps a solve with $D\dg$ directly onto one with $D$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:b5}). When $b_s$ and
$c_s$ depend on $s$, this relation no longer holds, and we use $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ as a
preconditioner for $D\dg$ (Secs.~\ref{sec:c4idea}--\ref{sec:g5fail}). Because this second route
needs only an approximate inverse of $D$, it applies to any preconditioner built for the unsquared
operator.

\subsection{\New \texorpdfstring{$D\dg$}{D-dagger} through \texorpdfstring{$D$}{D} itself}
\label{sec:b5}
\New Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:b5sim} of Sec.~\ref{sec:C} gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;(D\dg)^{-1}=\Gfive B_5\,D^{-1}B_5^{-1}\,\Gfive .\;}
\label{eq:b5}
\end{equation}
The solve of $D\dg w=r$ is then the solve of $Dx=B_5^{-1}\Gfive r$, and $w=\Gfive B_5x$.

Here $B_5$ plays the role that $D_-=cW-1$ plays in Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5exact}.
\New $D_-$ couples neighboring sites, so its inverse is a solve of its own (Sec.~\ref{sec:g5fail}).
$B_5$ is site-local and inverted exactly (Sec.~\ref{sec:C}), and by the argument of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappaC} (Appendix~\ref{sec:cgform})
\begin{equation}
\kappa(B_5)\le\frac{b+c}{b-c}=2\qquad(b=1.5,\ c=0.5).
\end{equation}

\New Equations~\eqref{eq:b5sim} and~\eqref{eq:b5} need $b$ and $c$ independent of $s$, as they are
here. With coefficients $b_s$, $c_s$ that depend on $s$,
$B_5=\operatorname{diag}(b_s)-\operatorname{diag}(c_s)\,Y$ with diagonal matrices in $s$. $Y$
shifts $s$ by one, so $\operatorname{diag}(b_s)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(c_s)$ commute with $Y$
only when they are multiples of the identity. Otherwise $B_5$ no longer commutes
with $1+Y$, and $\Gfive$ also turns $b_s$ into $b_{L_s-1-s}$. Coefficients that depend on $s$
are used in practice. The Zolotarev choice of optimal domain-wall fermions~\cite{ChiuZolo} depends on $s$,
and Yasunaga, Yoshimura, Tomiya and Nagai~\cite{YasunagaML} train $b_s$ and $c_s$ on each slice by
machine learning, with the residual mass as the loss, and find a smaller residual mass than with
constant coefficients. For such operators the solve with $D\dg$
needs the preconditioner of Sec.~\ref{sec:c4idea}.

On the even sites the relation carries over directly. Because $\Gfive$ and $B_5$ act locally within
each four-dimensional site, they commute with $\pi$ and $\iota$, yielding
\begin{equation}
S^{-\dagger}=\pi D^{-\dagger}\iota=\Gfive B_5\,S^{-1}B_5^{-1}\,\Gfive ,
\end{equation}
with $B_5$ restricted to the even sites. Since $B_5^{-1}\Gfive$ preserves support on the even
sites, the $D\dg$ solve in HMC reduces to a single even-site solve with $D$, employing the
identical cycle $\mathcal M$ and outer Krylov solver at essentially the same computational cost.

\subsection{\New A preconditioner for any coefficients}
\label{sec:c4idea}
For Wilson fermions, $\gamma_5$-Hermiticity ($D_W\dg=\gamma_5D_W\gamma_5$) immediately gives
$D_W^{-\dagger}=\gamma_5D_W^{-1}\gamma_5$, allowing any solver for $D_W$ to invert $D_W\dg$. For
M\"obius fermions this relation breaks down at $c\neq0$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:g5}); nonetheless, we may
still apply $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ as a preconditioner for $D\dg$. Because this construction
requires no algebraic identity between $D$ and $D\dg$, it remains applicable when
Eq.~\eqref{eq:b5} fails. For constant $b$ and $c$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:c4} below quantifies its
preconditioning quality.

Multiply $D\dg$ by $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ and use $D\dg=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,\Gfive D_-\dg$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5exact} and $\Gfive D_-\dg\Gfive=D_-$:
\begin{equation}
D\dg\,\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive=\Gfive D_-^{-1}D\,D_-D^{-1}\,\Gfive
=\Gfive\big(1+D_-^{-1}[D,D_-]D^{-1}\big)\Gfive .
\end{equation}
From $D=D_+-D_-Y$ and $[D_+,D_-]=0$, the commutator is
$[D,D_-]=-D_-[Y,D_-]=-c\,D_-[Y,W]$. The factor $D_-$ cancels, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;D\dg\,\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive=\Gfive\,(1-N)\,\Gfive,\qquad N=c\,[Y,W]\,D^{-1}.\;}
\label{eq:c4}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:c4} has three properties used below:
\begin{itemize}
\item $D_-^{-1}$ has dropped out, avoiding an additional solve with $D_-$ or $D_-\dg$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:g5fail}).
\item $[Y,W]=-\tfrac12[Y,K]$ is a bounded operator consisting of one hop in four dimensions and one in the
fifth. So $N$ is large only where $D^{-1}$ is large, on the low modes of $D$.
\item On a low mode $v$ the commutator acts through the $\gamma_\mu$ in $K$, which flip
chirality. A low mode of $D$ carries on each wall the chirality bound there, $\Pm$ at $s=0$ and
$\Pp$ at $s=L_s-1$; $[Y,W]v$ carries on each wall the opposite chirality, which no low mode has
there. \New So we expect $N$ to take the low modes out of their
span and $N^2$ to be small. Then $(1-N)^{-1}\approx1+N$, and a Krylov method would need only a few
steps more than for the identity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{On the even sites}
The solves of HMC are on the even sites, so we need the same idea for the Schur complement $S$.
The blocks of $D\dg$ are $\Ce\dg$, $\Co\dg$ on the diagonal and $D_{oe}\dg$, $D_{eo}\dg$ off it, so
its Schur complement is $S\dg$, and
$S^{-1}=\pi D^{-1}\iota$. Because $\Gfive$ acts within a site, it commutes with $\pi$ and
$\iota$, and
\begin{equation}
S^{-\dagger}=\pi D^{-\dagger}\iota\approx\pi\,\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive\,\iota=\Gfive S^{-1}\Gfive .
\end{equation}
\New The solve of $D\dg w=r$ eliminates the odd sites as in Sec.~\ref{sec:eo}, which
leaves $S\dg w_e=r'_e$ on the even sites. HMC already uses $\mathcal K=\Ce^{-1}S$, so we write
$w_e=(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z$; then $S\dg w_e=S\dg(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z=\mathcal K\dg z$, and the even-site system
is $\mathcal K\dg z=r'_e$. Its inverse is $(\mathcal K\dg)^{-1}=\Ce\dg S^{-\dagger}\approx
\Ce\dg\,\Gfive S^{-1}\Gfive$, which is the preconditioner. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dag} does these
steps in order. Its lines~3--4 apply the preconditioner with one cycle $\mathcal M$ of $\AR$ for
$S^{-1}$, using $S^{-1}t=\Ce^{-1}\AR^{-1}(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg t\approx\Ce^{-1}\mathcal M\,(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg t$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ARinv}.
Each GCR step costs one cycle, as a step of the solve with $D$ does.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Solve of $D\dg w=r$ with the cycle of $D$ (no second setup).}
\label{alg:dag}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State $r'_e\gets r_e-D_{oe}\dg(\Co\dg)^{-1}r_o$ \Comment{then $S\dg w_e=r'_e$}
\State solve $\mathcal K\dg z=r'_e$ by flexible GCR, whose preconditioner maps $t$ to
\State \quad $t_1\gets(\Spv\Cpve^{-1})\dg\,\Gfive t$;\quad $t_2\gets\Ce^{-1}\mathcal M\,t_1$ \Comment{$t_2\approx S^{-1}\Gfive t$}
\State \quad $\Ce\dg\,\Gfive t_2$ \Comment{$\approx\Ce\dg\Gfive S^{-1}\Gfive t$}
\State $w_e\gets(\Ce\dg)^{-1}z$
\State $w_o\gets(\Co\dg)^{-1}\big(r_o-D_{eo}\dg w_e\big)$
\State \Return $w=(w_e,w_o)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{\New Why we do not invert \texorpdfstring{$D_-$}{D-}}
\label{sec:g5fail}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5exact} seems to offer both the exact null vectors of $D\dg$ and an exact
reduction of $D\dg w=r$ to a solve with $D$. Both need $D_-^{-1}$. \New A Krylov method applied to $D_-$ itself converges poorly,
because of its spectrum. Since $D_-=cW-1=c\,(W-1/c)$, $D_-$ is $c$ times the
Wilson operator of mass $m_0=-M_5-1/c$, \New giving $-3$ in setup~S ($M_5=1.0$) and $-3.8$ in setup~B ($M_5=1.8$, Sec.~\ref{sec:setups}). The free
eigenvalues of that operator,
\begin{equation}
m_0+\sum_\mu(1-\cos p_\mu)\pm i\Big(\sum_\mu\sin^2p_\mu\Big)^{1/2},
\end{equation}
have real parts from $m_0$ to $m_0+8$, here from $-3$ to $5$ in setup~S and from $-3.8$ to $4.2$ in
setup~B. Near real part zero they have
imaginary parts of both signs, so the spectrum surrounds the origin. By the maximum principle
no polynomial $p$ with $p(0)=1$ is smaller than $1$ everywhere on a closed curve around the
origin, so a Krylov method reduces the residual only by resolving single eigenvalues, with a
degree of the order of their number (Sec.~\ref{sec:spectra}). CG on $D_-\dg D_-$ is not limited in
this way, so reducing $D\dg w=r$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5exact} ($D(\Gfive D_-\dg w)=D_-\Gfive r$)
remains possible, but requires an additional linear solve with $D_-\dg$ outside the solve with $D$
(and constructing null vectors from Eq.~\eqref{eq:g5exact} likewise requires solving with $D_-$).
Equations~\eqref{eq:b5} and~\eqref{eq:c4} need no such solve.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

We have presented an adaptive aggregation multigrid solver for the even-odd preconditioned
M\"obius domain-wall operator with a factorized Pauli--Villars coarse-grid correction. Evaluated on
four quenched $24^3\times48$ configurations on a single GPU at $m=0.001$, the solver achieves a
$6.4$--$10.9\times$ speed-up in solve time over mixed-precision even-odd CGNE
\New without deflation, and reduces the total wall-clock time of a 12-column
point-source propagator (including setup) to \New $0.27$--$0.49$ of the CGNE
time. Between $m=0.1$ and $m=0.001$, the multigrid iteration count grows by a factor of only
$2.0$--$2.4$, compared with $20$--$32$ for CGNE. Excluding setup, at $m=0.001$ the multigrid is
faster than CGNE from $24^4$ upward, while on $16^4$ the ranges across the four configurations
overlap in setup~S. Unpreconditioned BiCGStab and Chebyshev-preconditioned GCR(2) on
$\AL$ remain slower than mixed-precision CGNE, confirming that the acceleration originates from the
coarse-grid correction.

The design addresses the three points \New drawn from Boyle and Yamaguchi~\cite{BY} by
formulating the outer iteration on the even-odd system, collapsing the entire fifth dimension into
each four-dimensional aggregate, and adding coarse levels below the first to avoid explicit
coarse-grid deflation. Although the factorized correction is exact on the test vectors only for the
right-preconditioned form, omitting the Pauli--Villars lift $\hat t$ causes GCR(2) to stagnate or
increase its iteration count by several times on configurations in setup~S, and also degrades
convergence in setup~B. Replacing $\{s,\hat t\}$ with twice as many vectors $y$ lifted via $s$
alone lowers the iteration count on $24^3\times48$, but increases the setup time by a factor of
$1.2$--$1.7$. Because $\alpha$ does not enter the setup or coarse operators, one setup applies to
every value of Neff's $\alpha$.

Spectral analysis shows that the field of values of $\AR\mathcal M$ crosses into the left
half-plane in both setups, so the sufficient condition of Eisenstat, Elman, and Schultz~\cite{ESS}
for truncated GCR does not hold. \New With exact coarse solves,
the unit vector $x$ at the leftmost point of $W(\AR\mathcal M)$ gives $\Re\,x\dg\AR\mathcal Mx<0$.
With the coarse solves stopped at $10^{-2}$, the same quantity on the same $x$ is $0.66$--$1.02$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}). With the parameter choices of Table~\ref{tab:setups}, truncated GCR(2)
\New converged reliably in every solve with $D$ across all tested volumes, for
both random and point-source right-hand sides.

\New Finally, solves with $D\dg$ as required in HMC reuse the multigrid
hierarchy of $D$ without a second setup. When $b$ and $c$ are independent of $s$, the exact
identity of Sec.~\ref{sec:b5} maps a solve with $D\dg$ onto a solve with $D$, matching its
iteration count to within two iterations up to $24^3\times48$ (Table~\ref{tab:b5}). \New For
$s$-dependent coefficients, the cycle of $D$ conjugated by $\Gfive$ can serve as the preconditioner.
We tested it with constant $b$ and $c$. With GCR(2) it needs $2$ to $7$ more iterations than $D$
and stalls on the residual of $12^4$ in setup~S; GCR(8) removes the stall (Sec.~\ref{sec:daggerresults}).

\paragraph{Code.} All numerical results were generated with RutticeQCD
(\url{https://github.com/atsanroger/RutticeQCD}), a Rust reimplementation of
Bridge++~\cite{BridgeUeda,Bridge} whose GPU backend uses the Wilson and M\"obius CUDA kernels of
Bridge++. For every quantity shared with Bridge++ (the plaquette, Wilson, clover, and domain-wall
matrix-vector products, Krylov iteration counts, meson correlators, and single HMC trajectories),
the results have been verified against Bridge++ to numerical precision. The domain-wall multigrid
solver, which Bridge++ does not include, \New is checked through its true residual. Every solve stops
on $|D\psi-\eta|^2/|\eta|^2<10^{-18}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:outer}), computed with the operator $D$ that is
verified against Bridge++.

\section{\New Future work}
\label{sec:open}

\ifresults
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Parameters from the operator.} \New The smoother interval
$[\lambda_{\rm hi}/\kappa_s,\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ uses a ratio chosen per setup, and a wrong ratio
makes the solve stagnate (Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). The setup works with singular values of
$\AR$, which for a non-normal operator need not match its eigenvalues. Open questions are how to
set the interval from the operator, whether an ellipse with complex foci should replace the real
interval, and why setup~S needs more test vectors than setup~B (Sec.~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). The
low modes may vary in the fifth dimension more than vectors not split in $s$ can follow; Eick and
Mawhinney~\cite{EickMawhinney} find their fifth-dimensional profiles to differ between lattices.
A test vector split in $s$, as it is now split in chirality, would keep $s$ and $\hat t$ in the
range of $\Pro$, and L\"uscher's coherence test would measure what the coarse space captures.
Whyte, Stathopoulos and Romero~\cite{WhyteSVD} truncate the test vectors of Wilson fermions by a
singular value decomposition on each aggregate. Here the truncated basis would no longer contain
$s$ and $\hat t$ exactly, so the gain has to outweigh that loss.

\item \textbf{Convergence theory.} \New Two theoretical questions are open. The first is a
convergence theory for truncated GCR($k$) with a step-varying preconditioner, with a safeguard
against the fixed points of Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}. The second is why stout link smearing gives the
Galerkin coarse Pauli--Villars operator $\Pro\dg\Dpv\Pro$ singular values far smaller than those
of the fine operator $\Dpv$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:pvcoarse}). We also plan to follow the spectral
outliers and pseudospectra of setup~S beyond $16^4$.

with $24$\,GB, so this comparison needs a GPU with more memory.

The following questions of theory and algorithm remain open.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exactness for the left form.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact} holds
for the right form. For $\AL$ (with or without $\alpha$) the Pauli--Villars lift
that would be needed differs from $\hat t$ by site-local factors (Sec.~\ref{sec:exactness}). Whether a basis can be made exact for both forms at
once, and what is lost by using the right-form basis for the left form, remains to be seen.

\item \textbf{Smoother interval for a non-normal operator.} The interval
$[\lambda_{\rm hi}/\kappa_s,\lambda_{\rm hi}]$ is set from an estimate of the
spectral radius, whereas the setup works with singular values (eigenvalues of
$\AR\dg\AR$). For a non-normal $\tilde A$ these need not agree. Which part of
the low end is left to the smoother after the coarse correction, and whether an
ellipse with complex foci should replace the real interval, are not yet known.

\item \textbf{Even-odd smoother and coarse space.} Boyle and
Yamaguchi~\cite{BY} found that a red-black smoother combined with the HDCR
coarse correction was not effective, and attributed it to the eigenvectors of
the red-black operator and of $\gamma_5RD$ not being aligned. In the present
method the smoother and the correction both approximate $\tilde A^{-1}$ and the
coarse space is built from low modes of $\AR$, so that particular mismatch is
absent by construction; but the coarse operators are Galerkin projections of
$D$ and $\Dpv$; they are not projections of $\tilde A$. Whether the even-odd outer system is
preferable to the full-lattice $A'$ for this method has not been established.

\item \textbf{Field of values versus real parts of eigenvalues.}
Section~\ref{sec:spectra} shows $\Re\lambda>0$ for $A'$. The GCR bound~\cite{ESS}
needs a positive definite Hermitian part, i.e.\ a field of values in the right
half-plane, which is a stronger condition. Whether it holds for $A'$, $\AR$ and
$\AL$, and whether a shift of the kind used in Ref.~\cite{BoyleOare} is needed,
is not known.

\item \textbf{Outer solver with a varying preconditioner.} Because the coarse
solves are truncated, $\mathcal M$ is nonlinear and depends on the right-hand
side. FGMRES and flexible GCR allow this; BiCGStab does not, and its behavior
with $\mathcal M$ as preconditioner has no theoretical justification.

\item \textbf{Solving with $D\dg$.} Sec.~\ref{sec:dagger} uses $\Gfive D^{-1}\Gfive$ as a
preconditioner of $D\dg$; its error $N=c[Y,W]D^{-1}$ is argued to have a small square. A
bound on $\|N^2\|$ on the low modes has not been derived. The cycle with $D$ and $\Dpv$ exchanged
and its Pauli--Villars factor regularized also solves with $D\dg$ from the same $\Pro$, with coarse
operators of its own; its cost in HMC is left to that study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Krylov solvers and Chebyshev iteration}
\label{sec:cgform}

The multigrid uses four Krylov solvers, flexible GCR for the outer system, BiCGStab and CG for
the coarse factors and FGMRES in the bootstrap, together with Chebyshev iteration for the smoother.
The comparison uses CG as well.

\paragraph{Flexible GCR($k$).} The outer solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:outer} is GCR~\cite{ESS} with a
preconditioner $\mathcal M$ that may change from one step to the next, keeping the last $k$
directions. Each step applies $\mathcal M$ once, makes the new $q=\tilde Az$ orthogonal to the $q$
of the kept directions, and minimizes the residual along it; the step length $\langle q|r\rangle$
vanishes when $q$ is orthogonal to $r$, which is the fixed point of Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}. After
$n_{\max}$ steps the true residual is recomputed and the solve restarts.

\paragraph{FGMRES.} Each bootstrap round of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} replaces a vector $y_j$ by a few
steps of flexible GMRES~\cite{SaadFGMRES} on $\AR x=y_j$ from $x=0$, with the cycle as a right
preconditioner. The basis is orthogonalized by classical Gram--Schmidt applied twice, since one
pass loses orthogonality on the strongly non-normal $\AR\mathcal M$, and the least-squares problem
of the Hessenberg matrix is updated by Givens rotations at every step.

\paragraph{CGNE.} The reference solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:massscan} is CG on $\mathcal K\dg\mathcal K$ with
$\mathcal K=\Ce^{-1}S$, the even-odd operator of Bridge++, with Neff's $\alpha$ as for the
multigrid, in double or in mixed precision.

For $D\psi=\eta$ the even-odd reduction of Sec.~\ref{sec:eo} gives $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}y=Q_e^{-1}\Ce^{-1}\eta'_e$
with $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}=Q_e^{-1}\mathcal KQ_e$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ksimilar}, and CG solves its normal
equation $A y=b$, $A=\mathcal K^{(\alpha)\dagger}\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$,
$b=\mathcal K^{(\alpha)\dagger}Q_e^{-1}\Ce^{-1}\eta'_e$; \New $\psi_e=Q_ey$ and $\psi_o$ follow as for the
multigrid. \New CG stops when $|D\psi-\eta|^2/|\eta|^2<10^{-18}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:outer}). \New If its own residual
$|b-Ay|^2/|b|^2$ reaches its tolerance first, the tolerance is lowered and CG continues. Since the unknown
is $y$ itself and $A$ has $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$ on the right, Bridge++ and Ref.~\cite{Saad} call this method CGNR; we
keep the name CGNE that is common in lattice QCD.

\paragraph{Mixed precision.} The mixed-precision solver runs the CG recurrence in single precision
and corrects it by reliable updates~\cite{SleijpenvdV,ClarkMixed} in double precision. Each time
the single-precision $|\hat r|^2$ has fallen by a factor $\delta=10^{-2}$ below its largest value
since the last update, the single-precision solution is added to $y$ and the true residual $b-Ay$
is recomputed in double precision; CG then goes on with the same search direction and stops only on
the double-precision residual. Running the same CG loop entirely in double precision gives plain
double-precision CG; running it in single precision gives the solver of the Pauli--Villars factor,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tikhonov}, started from $x=0$. \New On a random right-hand side on $24^3\times48$, the
mixed-precision solver stays within $1\%$ of the double-precision iteration count, except at
$m\le0.002$ in setup~S, where it requires up to $67\%$ more iterations. It reaches the same final
residual in $0.53$--$0.88$ of the double-precision solve time.

\paragraph{BiCGStab.} The coarse solves of the quark factor (Sec.~\ref{sec:coarse}) use
BiCGStab~\cite{vanderVorst} with a right preconditioner $\mathcal M_A$. It uses the $\omega$
safeguard of Ref.~\cite{SleijpenvdVomega} with $\omega_c=0.7$, and after a breakdown
or $n_{\max}$ steps it restarts from the true residual.\ifresults{} The same algorithm, with
$\mathcal M_A=1$ and in double precision, is the Krylov solver on $\AL$ alone of
Sec.~\ref{sec:nomg}.\fi

\paragraph{Chebyshev iteration.} \New The smoother of Sec.~\ref{sec:smoother} evaluates
$x=p(\tilde A)b$ for $\tilde Ax=b$ on $[\lambda_{\rm lo},\lambda_{\rm hi}]$, with
$\bar\lambda=\frac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}+\lambda_{\rm lo})$,
$\delta=\frac12(\lambda_{\rm hi}-\lambda_{\rm lo})$ and $\sigma=\bar\lambda/\delta$, by the
Chebyshev recurrence~\cite{Saad}. Defining $\varrho_k=T_k(\sigma)/T_{k+1}(\sigma)$ and dividing
$T_{k+2}(\sigma)=2\sigma T_{k+1}(\sigma)-T_k(\sigma)$ by $T_{k+2}(\sigma)$ gives
$\varrho_{k+1}=1/(2\sigma-\varrho_k)$, starting from $\varrho_0=T_0(\sigma)/T_1(\sigma)=1/\sigma$.
With $x=0$, $r_0=b$ and $d_0=r_0/\bar\lambda$, step $k=0,\dots,\nu-1$ is
\begin{equation}
x\leftarrow x+d_k,\quad r_{k+1}=r_k-\tilde Ad_k,\quad
\varrho_{k+1}=\frac{1}{2\sigma-\varrho_k},\quad
d_{k+1}=\varrho_{k+1}\varrho_k\,d_k+\frac{2\varrho_{k+1}}{\delta}\,r_{k+1}.
\label{eq:cheb_rec}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Two even-odd forms.} Boyle and Yamaguchi~\cite{BY} used CG
on $S\dg S$, which for Shamir fermions needed 3224 iterations against 3880 for the left-preconditioned
form. Fig.~\ref{fig:cgform} compares the two on \New one configuration each of $12^4$, $16^4$ and $24^3\times48$
at $m=0.001$, in double precision with one random right-hand side, for $b-c=1$ and five
values of $c$; all other parameters are those of setups S and B.

The two operators differ by a site-local factor on the left, $S=\Ce\mathcal K$, and with Neff's
$\alpha$, $SQ_e=\Ce Q_e\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$. Their condition numbers therefore differ at most by
the condition number of that factor. Since $C=d_++d_-Y$ with $\|Y\|=1$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:D}), the
singular values of $C$ lie in $[d_+-|d_-|,\,d_++|d_-|]$ and
\begin{equation}
\kappa(C)\le\frac{d_++|d_-|}{d_+-|d_-|},\qquad d_+=1+b(4-M_5),\quad d_-=1-c(4-M_5);
\label{eq:kappaC}
\end{equation}
for $\alpha<1$ the bound is $1/\alpha$ times larger, since $\kappa(Q)=1/\alpha$. The measured
ratios of the iterations stay within this range too, although the bound is on the condition
numbers.

Within the bound the order depends on $c$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cgform}). In setup~B the ratio of
the iterations grows smoothly with $c$ and crosses $1$ near the $(b,c)=(1.5,0.5)$ of the main text.
For Shamir fermions \New in setup~B $S\dg S$ is faster by a factor $1.21$--$1.23$, in agreement with the
$3880/3224=1.20$ of Ref.~\cite{BY}. In setup~S the ratio is above $1$ except at $c=0.25$. The three
lattices give the same ratios, so the order depends on the operator and its parameters and not on
the configuration.

With $\alpha=0.6$, $S\dg S$ needs more iterations than $\mathcal K\dg\mathcal K$ for every $c$ on
all three lattices, by about half in setup~S. In $\mathcal K^{(\alpha)}$ the factor $\Ce Q_e$ is
divided out on the left, whereas in $SQ_e$ the rescaling acts on the right only. For the setups of this work $\mathcal K\dg\mathcal K$ is the faster form, and the multigrid is
compared with it.

\section{\New Spectrum and pseudospectra of the cycle}
\label{sec:spectral}
With exact coarse solves the cycle $\mathcal M$ is a fixed linear map (Sec.~\ref{sec:why}). For a
fixed map $X$, the \emph{$\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum} is the set of $z$ where
$\sigma_{\min}(z-X)<\varepsilon$~\cite{NRT,TrefethenEmbree}. \New For a normal $X$ it is the
$\varepsilon$-neighborhood of the spectrum; for a non-normal $X$ it can be much larger. The condition
number of an eigenvalue, $\kappa=1/|y\dg x|$ for its normalized right and left eigenvectors, is $1$
for a normal matrix and widens the pseudospectrum around the eigenvalue. The spectrum and the
pseudospectra of $X=\AR\mathcal M$ bound full GMRES with the fixed cycle~\cite{TrefethenEmbree}; they do
not cover truncated GCR(2) with inexact coarse solves (Sec.~\ref{sec:gcrk}).

\subsection{Spectrum}
\label{sec:spectrum}

Although $W(\AR\mathcal M)$ crosses into the left half-plane (Sec.~\ref{sec:why}), the eigenvalues of
$\tilde A\mathcal M$ remain well separated from the origin (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}). \New We compute
them by Krylov--Schur at $m=0.001$ and $\alpha=1$, with exact coarse solves and the Pauli--Villars
factor regularized. The smallest real part is $0.869$ (S) and $0.837$ (B) on configuration~1 of
$12^4$, and $0.728$--$0.806$ (S) and $0.752$--$0.804$ (B) over the four configurations of $16^4$.
The eigenvalues of smallest real part have condition numbers $\kappa=1/|y\dg x|$ from $3.0$ to
$36.8$, with left eigenvectors from $\mathcal M\dg\tilde A\dg$. In setup~S a few eigenvalues lie
farther from $1$, at $1.83+0.06i$ ($\kappa=9.0$) on $12^4$ in the right form, and at $2.94+0.05i$ and $1.93$ on
configuration~1 of $16^4$. On configuration~1 all other eigenvalues lie in $|\lambda-1|\le0.45$ (S) and $\le0.63$ (B),
a disc that excludes the origin. The left form gives nearly the same spectrum. On $12^4$ its
smallest real part is $0.862$ (S) and $0.834$ (B).

\subsection{Pseudospectra and a bound for GMRES}
\label{sec:pseudo}

\New For a non-normal operator $X$ whose field of values contains the origin, the convergence of
full GMRES is controlled by its $\varepsilon$-pseudospectrum. If a contour $\Gamma$ of length $L$
encloses the spectrum of $X$ while excluding the origin, and $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)\ge\varepsilon$
everywhere on $\Gamma$, then for any polynomial $p_k$ satisfying $p_k(0)=1$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\|p_k(X)\|\le\frac{L}{2\pi\varepsilon}\max_{z\in\Gamma}|p_k(z)| .
\label{eq:pseudobound}
\end{equation}
An isolated outlier $\lambda_o$ outside $\Gamma$ is removed by the factor $(1-z/\lambda_o)$ of
$p_k$, so that $p_k(X)=p_k(X)(I-P_o)$ with $P_o$ the spectral projector and
$\|(z-X)^{-1}(I-P_o)\|\le\kappa_o/\varepsilon$. On a circle $|z-c|=r$ with
$p_k(z)=(1-z/\lambda_o)(1-z/c)^{k-1}$, this gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\|r_0\|}\le\kappa_o\,\frac{r}{\varepsilon}\,
\frac{|\lambda_o-c|+r}{|\lambda_o|}\Big(\frac rc\Big)^{k-1}.
\label{eq:outlierbound}
\end{equation}
When the circle is sampled at points $z_i$ with spacing $h$, because $\sigma_{\min}(z-X)$ is
$1$-Lipschitz in $z$, the lower bound $\varepsilon=\min_i\sigma_{\min}(z_i-X)-h/2$ holds on the
whole circle.

\New On $12^4$ (configuration~1), these bounds guarantee that full GMRES with a fixed cycle reaches
$\|r_k\|/\|r_0\|<10^{-9}$ within $55$ iterations in setup~S and $57$ in setup~B
(Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}). In setup~S, the circle $|z-1|=0.6$ sampled at $160$ points gives
$\min_i\sigma_{\min}=0.0180$ and $\varepsilon\ge0.0062$; with the outlier $\lambda_o=1.83+0.06i$
($\kappa_o=9.0$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:outlierbound} gives rate $r/c=0.6$ and prefactor $682$. In setup~B,
the circle $|z-1.4|=0.93$ at $96$ points gives $\min_i\sigma_{\min}=0.1144$ and
$\varepsilon\ge0.0840$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:pseudobound} gives rate $r/c=0.66$ and prefactor $11.1$.
